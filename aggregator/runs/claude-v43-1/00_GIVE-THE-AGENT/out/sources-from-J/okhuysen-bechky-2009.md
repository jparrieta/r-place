Coordination, the process of interaction that integrates a collective set of
interdependent tasks, is a central purpose of organizations. In this review we
begin by discussing the origins of interest in coordination, tracing some of the
classic perspectives. We present a review of recent literature on coordination
in organizations arranged according to the mechanisms that help achieve it.
We then go beyond this review to provide a framework to understand what
different coordination mechanisms and activities accomplish. We propose
that coordination mechanisms (such as routines, meetings, plans, and schedules) impact the work of organizations by creating three integrative conditions
for coordinated activity: accountability, predictability, and common understanding. We end by examining the implications of such a perspective for
future research on coordination in organizations.

Introduction
After many years of limited activity, the last two decades have seen a
resurgence of interest in the topic of coordination in organizations. As a
consequence, we now have a vibrant and rich body of knowledge about how
people collectively perform their interdependent work. These findings
usefully update our image of coordination to reflect newer forms of work and
organizing. However, the new wave of research spans multiple disciplines and
pays attention to different aspects of the problem of coordination, leaving us
without a coherent framework. It is time to bring the wide-ranging new
understandings of coordination back into the fold of organizational theory by
taking stock of this area.
Our literature review has three main goals. We begin by presenting a brief
historical account of early perspectives on coordination in organizations
including the work of Frederick Taylor, Henry Fayol, and of organizational
design scholars. Although some of this early research has been disregarded
as quaint and outdated, it is in fact quite relevant to our understanding of
coordination and organizations. The coordination practices (such as the use
of time as an organizing structure) and concepts proposed by early theorists
(such as specialization and management control) continue to frame many of
the concerns addressed in contemporary research. Moreover, much of the
recent literature is a response to the shortcomings of these historical perspectives, and so a reminder of this research establishes the necessary context
for understanding the complementary role of contemporary treatments of
coordination in organizations. After touching on the historical highlights, we
proceed with our review of recent research by showcasing its unique contributions to our understanding of coordination. Finally, after acknowledging
some of the limits and challenges in this most recent wave of research, we
offer a perspective to frame coordination research in a more general manner
and to help advance our understanding of coordination in organizations.
As we delved into the literature for this review, we saw a need to establish
some limits to our work. For example, we found that the term “coordination”
was used ubiquitously and loosely to treat a variety of phenomena. In some
cases coordination is embedded in the treatment of other constructs, such as
collaboration and cooperation. And while collaboration and cooperation are
indeed indispensable in coordinated activity (Smith, Carroll, & Ashford,
1995), they are conceptually separate ideas. Thus, to delineate a boundary for
our review we began by focusing our attention on research that explains how
collective work is accomplished within organizations. This research is closely
grounded in work settings and seeks to explain the ways in which collective
action on a task takes place. In a few instances we also extended the boundary
to include research that provided important knowledge on how or why
coordination occurs.

Bounding our review in this way also leads us to exclude other literatures
that, although concerned with coordination, do not directly focus on the work
of organizations. For example, we exclude the extensive literature on coordination in experimental economics (Davis & Holt, 1993), research focused on
coordination between organizations or across organizational divisions (Sinha
& Van de Ven, 2005), and research that explores how markets can assist in
coordination of action (Williamson, 2005). Although these literatures touch
on related questions about coordination, the conditions under which these
types of coordination take place and the assumptions made in the research are
substantially different from coordination within a single organization that is
focused on performing its work.
We therefore begin with a look back at the emergence of coordination
demands in the railroads of the nineteenth century, some of the first largescale organizations in modern times (Chandler, 1962). We then describe the
role that scientific management played in early thinking about coordination
(Fayol, 1949; Taylor, 1916) and the evolution of work arrangements (Jacoby,
1984), followed by a description of the ideas proposed by organizational
design scholars (Hickson, Pugh, & Pheysey 1969; Thompson, 1967; Woodward, 1970). In explaining these perspectives we focus on the contributions
they have made to our understanding of coordination in organizations, as well
as their limitations. After presenting the historical background which frames
modern research on coordination in organizations, we proceed with our
review of the current literature.
Historical Background on Coordination
The advent of railroads in the eighteenth century engendered considerable
interest in coordination. Chandler (1962, p. 23) argued that railroads were the
first of a new breed of organization due to their size and complexity. Their
emergence and growth created a pressing need for large-scale coordination
because railroads needed to ensure that practical concerns, such as loading
passengers, delivering cargo, and avoiding crashes with other trains, could be
accomplished. The mechanism that enabled railroads to coordinate on a large
scale was the printed timetable (Stover, 1970). Timetables, for example,
allowed a train to “own” pieces of track at a specific time, which prevented
collisions. Through the use of timetables the railroads could coordinate
passengers, cargo, and other trains to meet a specific train when it was at a
station.
Timetables could only be used to coordinate trains, though, thanks to the
invention of Railroad Standard Time. This artificial creation divided the continental US into four different zones within which time was the same (Daniels,
2000; Stover, 1970). Railroad standard time replaced the over 500 local times
that existed in 1883: these exhibited uniqueness across different geographical
longitudes because true time was measured using clocks calibrated to the local

The Design of Work
The formal study of coordination began shortly after the appearance of railroads, with the emergence of large-scale manufacturing (Scott & Davis, 2007).
In the late nineteenth and early twentieth century, manufacturing employment rose dramatically, along with the size of manufacturing establishments
(Jacoby, 1984). With increases in scale and speed of production the old “drive
system” of management, in which foremen pressured workers to increase
output, was no longer an efficient way to organize work. Not only was this
system arbitrary and unfair to workers, but foremen were typically bad record
keepers and disorganized managers, limiting the potential benefits of such a
system (Jacoby, 1984).
The problems inherent in the drive system served as the impetus for the
development of schemes to maximize efficiency and better coordinate
through the design of work (Chandler, 1962). One of the most famous
methods to maximize efficiency was scientific management, which operated
by examining the work that was being performed and decomposing it into
its most basic elements, thereby allowing for specialization and the reduction
or elimination of waste. This approach, perhaps best exemplified by the
engineering methods of Frederick Taylor as well as Lillian and Frank
Gilbreth, attempted to rationalize work and find the best way of accomplishing it. Their methods focused on the proper design of tools, fixtures, and even
the raw materials for manufacturing through standardization and the interchangeability of parts. In addition, scientific management also advocated the
precise and rational use of workers within the manufacturing setting (Taylor,
1916).
The implementation of rationalization in manufacturing through specialization, though, also created additional demands for approaches to bring
together the inputs from specialized tasks into an integrated output. Scott and
Davis (2007) illustrate the consequences that specialization can have when
they note that the adoption of specialized (and improved) tools can lead to
increased demands for communication. To ensure that the right tools are
available in the right place at the right time for the work to be performed,
organizations have to improve scheduling and other allocation mechanisms.
With the rationalization of manufacturing work through its design, we begin

The Design of Organizations
Later scholars took a different perspective on coordination through an
emphasis on the design of management systems rather than the design of
work. Perhaps the best known management scholar to approach the problem
of rationalizing organizations was Henry Fayol, who was inspired by Frederick Taylor (Fayol, 1949). For Fayol, the rationalization of organizations began
with proper design. In his view, work could only be accomplished if the right
management systems were put in place. Fayol’s perspective was guided by
principles for hierarchical systems, such as the unity of command, centralization, and the subordination of individual interests. Fayol (1949) argued that,
in contrast to Taylor’s “bottom–up” approach, his was a “top–down”
approach, one that ensured that the central element in administration, unity
of command, was respected.
Planning formal elements of organizations also featured prominently in
organizational design, where scholars tried to determine how to devise organizations according to the contingencies they faced in their environment.
Coordination, particularly across departments and functions, was a key outcome of these models. Several organizational design theories focused on
uncertainties rooted in the task or technology such as production technology
(Woodward, 1970), task complexity (Perrow, 1967), workflow integration
(Hickson, Pugh, & Pheysey, 1969), and task interdependence (Thompson,
1967). These uncertainties influenced the structure of coordination in organizations: with more variable or uncertain technology, organizations relied less
on impersonal structures like hierarchy, rules, programs, and more on interpersonal communication or feedback (Van de Ven, Delbecq, & Koenig, 1976;
Argote, 1982).
A significant characteristic of the approaches advocated by Taylor, the
Gilbreths, Fayol, and organizational design was the deliberate nature of their
attempts to plan systems to produce coordinated activity. This deliberate formalization occurred either through the design of work (Taylor, 1916) or the
design of relationships between positions in the organization (Fayol, 1949). In
essence, coordination resulted from the specification of exchanges between
areas of work through roles, rules, and structures (physical and organizational). The central assumption of these approaches was designability: these
scholars believed that organizational systems could be articulated with enough
specificity and precision to allow for individuals to fully complete the work.
Such formalization continues to be seen as an essential feature of organizations, in which the “rules governing behavior are precisely and explicitly formulated”, and “roles and role relations are prescribed independently of the
personal attributes and relations of individuals” (Scott & Davis, 2007, p. 29).

These theories, heavily reliant on design, were developed in the era in
which manufacturing and production work was the dominant form. As a consequence, some of the distinctive characteristics of this context, such as the
tangibility of its products, influenced the theorizing and conceptualization of
coordination. For example, in most manufacturing operations the product of
the work is visible and measurable, so progress towards the task and its
completion can be monitored directly. Similarly, the physical nature of a
manufactured product means that the process to create it can generally be
examined, rationalized, and decomposed into sub-elements which can be
integrated to complete the product through the use of assembly lines. The
objects of manufacture, themselves, can aid in making the interdependencies
between different groups evident. The physical nature of a manufactured
product also means that problems can be more easily identified, and thus
resolved. Lastly, in early manufacturing settings many of the interdependent
parties were co-located, making everyone’s work visible, enabling problem
resolution across groups.
As the nature of work has changed in organizations due to the shift away
from manufacturing, the limitations of these coordination theories have
become evident. In many instances, organizations provide services rather than
products, the nature of which is less tangible (Davis, 2003). In other instances,
such as the creative work involved in product or software design, a single optimal solution may not exist and progress towards completion may be difficult
to estimate (Kraut & Streeter, 1995). Moreover, as the boundaries of organizations have become blurred, the difference between what lies at the technical
core of the organization and the periphery has become ambiguous (Scott,
2004; Hargadon, 2003). In each of these cases, interdependencies between
different pieces of work may be uncertain or challenging to identify, making it
difficult to know who should be involved in work and whether there is a correct order in which parties should complete their own specialized work.
Another shortcoming of the earlier coordination literature is the portrayal
of processes and structures as formal elements planned by organizations
rather than as ongoing work activities that emerge in response to coordination
challenges. This limitation is important because formalized, designed,
planned, or engineered solutions often fail to account for the many eventualities that organizations face in the performance of their work. In theories of
organizational design the informal behavior necessary to deal with such
unplanned contingencies was typically collected into a catch-all category
labeled “mutual adjustment” (Thompson, 1967), “lateral relations” (Galbraith,
1973), or “ad hoc coordination” (Donaldson, 2001). Because this category was
difficult for scholars to measure, these informal and emergent coordination
practices in organizations remained largely unexamined. This is unfortunate
because unplanned contingencies and the responses to them represent
important influences in organizations.

The literature on coordination that has developed in the last two decades
has taken great leaps to address these shortcomings in our understanding of
coordination. In addition to examining the formal and designed aspects of
coordination, contemporary researchers have also devoted considerable
attention to what was labeled “informal behavior” in earlier work. By taking
into account both the nature of post-industrial work and the emergent nature
of coordination, these new conceptions are beginning to remedy the outstanding gaps in our understanding of coordination in organizations.

Recent Approaches to Coordination
Recent research on coordination has extended beyond organizational theory
into a variety of disciplines such as computer science, information systems,
and sociology of work. Our review of this newer work leads us to agree that “at
its core, coordination is about the integration of organizational work under
conditions of task interdependence and uncertainty” (Faraj & Xiao, 2006,
p. 1156). While new research on coordination shares with earlier research an
interest in the tasks carried out by organizations, the approach to analyzing
these tasks differs. As we have noted, early work by efficiency experts and
organization design theorists rested on the assumption that organizational
arrangements can be designed for optimum performance. In contrast, new
research on coordination instead explores how the work of such arrangements
is executed. A difference in the recent research is that it is less concerned with
optimizing structures for a given environment, and instead considers coordination as it happens, assuming that people in organizations must coordinate
the work regardless of the organizational design.
The expansion of coordination research into new arenas has engendered a
greater range of theoretical and empirical concerns, resulting in new conceptions of and labels for coordination. For example, research in this domain has
focused on communicating (Ballard & Seibold, 2003), cooperation (Pinto,
Pinto, & Prescott, 1993), knowledge sharing (Bechky, 2003a; Carlile, 2002),
and interaction (Heath, Hindmarsh, & Luff, 1999). Moreover, scholars in the
recent wave of research have proposed a variety of definitions for the term
“coordination” which address their particular theoretical conclusions (Table
10.1). The multiple definitions, while demonstrating some variation due to
their range of focal interests, still embody evident commonalities: (1) people
work collectively; (2) the work is interdependent; and (3) a goal, task, or piece
of work is achieved.
Given the variety of definitions, for the purposes of our review, we will
adopt Faraj and Xiao’s (2006, p. 1157) definition of coordination as a “temporally unfolding and contextualized process of input regulation and interaction
articulation to realize a collective performance”. This definition best fits the
spirit of this recent wave of coordination research, reflecting scholars’ shared
interest in the emergent nature of the process of coordination. For instance,

Kellogg, Orlikowski and Yates (2006, p. 28) describe the coordination
practices used in an online advertising firm to “facilitate their dynamic, uncertain, and ongoing accommodation to each other and clients”. Similarly, Faraj
and Xiao (2006) demonstrate how dialogic coordination, time-sensitive

cases, coordination is accomplished on the ground as the work progresses.
Scholars are also studying cases where organization design is expected to fall
short. As Majchrzak, Jarvenpaa, and Hollingshead (2007, p. 147) point out,
when disasters strike, formal structures and planned responses are “too slow,
disconnected, and inadequate for the task”, and emergent group responses are
necessary to coordinate disaster relief. By transcending the assumptions of
planned approaches, this work shows that understanding emergent action in
context is critical to developing a full picture of coordination.
Our initial goal in our literature review is to bring together the varying
perspectives on emergent and contextualized coordination processes. To
organize this research, we first focused on the ways in which authors suggested that coordination is enabled or accomplished in the organizations they
studied. This, of course, raised some challenges: the development of a contextualized perspective in this research across different disciplines generated not
only different definitions, but a messy theoretical picture. We therefore sought
to find a way to reconcile the conflicting definitions, bring together the
disparate findings, and untangle the theoretical explanations to provide an
overarching framework.
Our criticisms of the literature and our ultimate unifying framework will
come later. First, however, we will start our assessment by analyzing the ways
in which coordination is accomplished and surveying the literature through
the lens of the mechanisms that enable coordination: processes such as routines or meetings, as well as artifacts such as schedules and blueprints, and
other methods. To do this, we classify the tools, technologies, and interactions
that are represented in recent research. We present this simplification as a
stepping stone to develop a deeper understanding of how coordination
processes operate.
Mechanisms for Coordination
Coordination mechanisms are the organizational arrangements that allow
individuals to realize a collective performance. Mintzberg (1989, p. 101)
suggests that coordination mechanisms are “the most basic elements of structure” in organizations and include both formal and emergent elements. In the
literature, these organizational arrangements often involve tools, technologies,
or interactions that bring interdependent elements together. For example,
they include arrangements like assembly lines, which bring materials to workers in a defined sequence, and roles, which can define the responsibilities of

Plans and Rules
Plans and rules, conceptualized as purposive elements of formal organizations, are deemed indispensable to organizing (March & Simon, 1958; Scott &
Davis, 2007). In early conceptions of organizations, managers were seen as
responsible for the creation of these plans, whether large-scale and strategic or
smaller and tactical, after which they were handed down a hierarchy to be
implemented by those lower in the organization. Rules are complementary,
because they establish relationships between different parts of the organization and serve to guide choices between alternatives when conflicts appear.
These perspectives on plans and rules are evident, in particular, in Taylor’s
(1914) and Fayol’s (1949) work. Within the new literature on coordination,
plans and rules are presented in a narrower way, with an emphasis on
prospective preparation for task completion.
Defining responsibility for tasks. Perhaps the most straightforward way in
which plans and rules coordinate is by explaining the actions that different
parties have to take to complete a task. For example, in trauma centers, coordination takes place through the reliance on protocols and rules (Faraj & Xiao,
2006), which incorporate the knowledge of different experts involved in
patient care and establish the sequences of activities for providing it. Pinto
et al. (1993), in their examination of project-based teams at hospitals, also
noted that some aspects of the work are controlled through plans and rules.
These reflect “the degree to which activities or tasks on the project team were
mandated or controlled”. Plans and rules can be developed at the organizational or team level. Plans and rules act by making it easier for people to relate
to each other and to respond to work as it progresses.
Resource allocation. Plans and rules are also useful in coordination
because they can ameliorate problems that stem from interdependence and be
used to match scarce resources to the tasks that require completion (Crowston, 1997). For this purpose, schedules are often used. Schedules place activities on a temporal map, using prearranged points as timing references (Ballard
& Seibold, 2003). For example, scheduling in a hospital follows rules to ensure
that the same operating room is not scheduled for two simultaneous surgeries
(Bardram, 2000), attempting to resolve the very real limitation of using the
same space at the same time for two different purposes. By following such
rules in the development of schedules, a hospital can ensure both appropriate
usage of limited resources and avoid any double-booking of resources that can
lead to conflict and disruption.

Developing agreement. Plans and rules can also be useful for coordination
through the agreement that they engender among parties. Bechky and Okhuysen (2009) show that plans, such as schedules and location maps, are created in
both a SWAT team and film production crews through an iterative process. The
interaction required to create schedules and maps highlights conflicts, discrepancies, and other difficulties in performing the work. The final product embodies a working agreement of how the mission or project will unfold, and serves
as an orienting device for the work of the different individuals in these groups.
As Pinto et al. (1993) note in project teams, these group-level plans and rules
can increase the level of cooperation across individuals in the group.

Objects and Representations
Scholars studying the use of technologies, objects, and other representations
show that they are useful for coordinating work activities. Rafaeli and VilnaiYafetz (2004) theorize that objects have three functions in organizations—
instrumental, symbolic, and aesthetic. Because coordination is primarily an
instrumental function in organizations, this property of objects is most often
emphasized in the literature. Conceptually, the literature that explains how
representations coordinate work draws on the idea of boundary objects from
science studies (Star & Griesemer, 1989). Boundary objects “inhabit” several
intersecting social worlds and satisty the information requirement of each of
them (Star & Griesemer, 1989, p. 393). Boundary objects can be used across
social worlds to convey both technical and social information and mobilize
action (Henderson, 1999; Star & Griesemer, 1989) because knowledge and
social dynamics inhere in material objects (Winner, 1980; Latour, 1988, 1996).
Objects and representations therefore coordinate by providing information.
They also offer a common referent around which people interact, align their
work, and create shared meaning.
Direct information sharing. Representation technologies enable direct
information sharing across groups in a variety of settings, from advertising to
medicine to engineering and navigation teams. For example, NASA engineers
working in a war-room environment use data spreadsheets to share information
that is necessary for the work to progress across different teams (Mark, 2002),
and teams on naval ships use a variety of technologies, from algorithms to simple
logs, to represent navigational problems in ways that make it easier for the sailors
to see and understand others’ activities (Hutchins, 1995). Each of these technologies provides a common space for interdependent groups to post information that is vital for the current task. Posting information also makes the activities
of the different groups visible, which helps coordinate their future work.
Scaffolding. Representations are sometimes created by groups to impose
order by providing a scaffolding or structure for activities. People use this

Acknowledging and aligning work. As both Bechky (2003a, 2003b) and
Carlile (2002) demonstrate, boundary objects are useful for solving problems
that occur between groups in production organizations, because when people
from those groups come together to interact about the work, such objects
allow them to acknowledge their progress on the task. For instance, Henderson (1991) shows how drawings and prototypes help design and production
engineers advance the introduction of a new turbine engine by mobilizing
discussion and action. Moreover, Carlile (2002) demonstrates that engineers
use objects to make their interdependencies clearer while designing automobiles. The specialized groups involved in these activities interact around
objects, which enable them to align their activities with one another and
acknowledge the work that is being done.
Creating a common perspective. By facilitating learning across groups,
objects and representations can assist in the creation of shared understandings
of the work process across organizations. These shared understandings help
coordinate the work. For instance, a representation such as a patient protocol
helps to make medical practices more concrete, as Faraj and Xiao (2006) point
out. By sharing protocols, those involved with treatment develop “a common
mental model of patient condition and treatment options” (Faraj & Xiao, 2006,
p. 116). Representations can help group members develop a broadly shared view
of the work and tasks the entire group will carry out. Bailetti, Callahan and DiPietro (1994) show that in chip design and manufacture, “coordination ensembles”—which are maps of interdependencies between tasks and group
members—help create a common view of the project that aids in coordination.
Roles
Scholars have also identified roles as a useful means for coordinating work
activity. Roles represent expectations associated with social positions, and
therefore can facilitate continuity of behavior over time (Hughes, 1958; Biddle
& Thomas, 1966). At the same time, roles can be loosely and dynamically
structured, as expectations are negotiated in interaction (Turner, 1986). This

coordinate across people, and negotiating behaviors in a role guides people in

and updating is one of the traditional functions of a formal hierarchy. When
subordinates report their activities to their supervisors, or supervisors check in
on their subordinates, they are coordinating the tasks of the group. In ER teams,
for instance, the hierarchy between attending, resident and student doctors
coordinates the work—the attending physicians monitor how those beneath
them progress on the case and interrupt or jump in if the work is not progressing
correctly (Klein, Ziegert, Knight, & Xiao, 2006). The overlapping roles of medical professionals in trauma teams also allow for what Faraj and Xiao (2006) call
“plug and play teaming”. In these teams professional control, roles, and hierarchy give people the tools to monitor the progress of the tasks and to keep
members accountable to one another for their activities, coordinating the work.
Substitution. Roles help create shared understandings of the responsibilities for tasks. For example, when individuals understand the tasks linked to
each others’ roles, they can substitute for one another in task execution. Such
substitution can be seen in ship navigation, where teammates such as the plotter and bearing timer-recorder’s shared understanding of tasks enables them
to fill in for others (Hutchins, 1991). This understanding of others’ roles and
their tasks are often developed informally. Bechky (2006) highlights that in the
film industry individuals early in their careers spend time rotating through
different departments. This allows them to develop common understandings
of the task requirements of different roles and how they relate to one another.
Thus, although film crew members work on many short-term projects with
different individuals filling different roles, they have strong shared understandings of the tasks performed by each role that enables immediate coordination from the first day of shooting.
Creating a common perspective. Research also shows that particular intergroup roles are important for sharing information about the work process
across the organization. For instance, Gittell’s (2002) study of a hospital
suggests that boundary spanners in groups can provide information about

coordination by making clear what tasks remain to be completed. A similar role

Routines
As with plans and rules, routines have been a part of conceptions of organizations at least since March and Simon (1958) discussed them. Routines can be
defined as “repeated patterns of behaviour that are bound by rules and
customs” (Feldman, 2000, p. 611). In early conceptions, routines were sometimes viewed as mechanistic properties of organizations, as stable and difficult
to change. Routines have been treated as stores of knowledge (Nelson &
Winter, 1982) and as the outcome of habitual behavior (Gersick & Hackman,
1990). More recently, Feldman (2000, 2004) and her colleagues (Feldman &
Pentland, 2003; Feldman & Rafaeli, 2002) have conducted research that moves
away from simple conceptualizations of routines, focusing instead on the
complex ways in which social meaning and social interaction are embedded
within them. This interpretive and emergent conception of routines has
expanded our understanding of the ways in which they coordinate. Routines
coordinate by providing a template for task completion, by bringing people
together, and by creating a common perspective across groups.
Task completion/stability. Routines help coordinate by making completion of the task visible. In the simplest manner, when they establish a sequence
of activities to be performed, routines enact a way for interdependent parties
to observe progress on the task. For example, Feldman (2000) notes how a
housing office performs its work in cycles lasting a full year. Some of the task
activities performed within that office include the selection and hiring of
resident advisors and organizing move-in weekends. By having these routines
in place, the parties involved in their execution can split the tasks into subtasks
and broadly anticipate who will be participating in completing the task at a
given time. Importantly, though, the performance of each instance of a
routine can be different from the last because these routines are executed once
a year, in many cases by different personnel, with changing objectives, and
with a constant emphasis on learning and improvement. This means that
while it is possible to expect that a given activity will take place or a given
objective will be met, it is harder to predict the details of the work performed
to complete those activities or achieve the objectives.
Hand-off work. Another way in which routines operate as coordinating
mechanisms is by establishing how work moves from one group to another to
complete an interdependent task. For example, Symons, Long and Ellis (1996)
describe how a “procedure trajectory” in a radiology department establishes
how to move patients from department to department, and how to track this

movement through reports. Similarly, Gittell (2002) finds that the specification
of task sequences through “clinical pathways”, the formal procedures in routine
joint replacement surgery, also coordinates action. In an advertising firm,
Kellogg et al. (2006) note a similar routine in which workers use a set of procedures to exchange files of their work to revise them for presentations to clients.
In each of these cases, a central feature of the mechanism used is the ability to
determine when one group has completed its work and another takes over.
Bringing groups together. Routines work as coordination mechanisms, in
part, by providing moments for interdependent parties to act jointly on a task.
For example, Feldman (2000) shows how bringing the athletic department
into a university’s move-in routine (by having them provide ice cream to the
new students) makes the athletics department more aware of the challenges
faced by the housing department. In particular, it makes it evident that if
athletic games are scheduled during the move-in weekend, the move-in
process for students is made more complicated. Feldman and Rafaeli (2002)
argue that as routines create connections among interacting parties in organizations, the parties are guided through their mutual interdependence. The
connections that are embodied in the routine, by facilitating interactions
among individuals, make progress on the task possible.
Creating a common perspective. Routines can also function as coordinating mechanisms as they help establish a common perspective on the work to be
done by a group. For example, Edmondson, Bohmer and Pisano (2001) examine how groups build routines for cardiac surgery through training after new
technology is introduced into a team. They note that some groups elaborate on
the “shared meaning” of the task through reflective practices in the process of
training, which lead to better subsequent performance in the operating room.
In particular, for these teams understanding the distribution of task knowledge
among individuals and how to use the new technologies are critical elements.
Similarly, Okhuysen (2005) explains that for police SWAT teams’ group training sessions, in which they rehearse routines to execute work, are a useful mechanism to establish a commonality of perspective towards the task.
Proximity
The final mechanism that supports coordination within organizations is
people’s physical proximity to one another. Distance has an influence on the
amount of interaction and communication between people in organizations
(Allen, 1977). For instance, early studies showed that people’s friendship,
marriage choices, and general attraction to others were related to their distance
in space (Festinger, Schacter, & Back, 1950; Festinger, 1951; Newcomb, 1956).
Within organizations, proximity’s influence on communication and liking is
mediated by visibility and familiarity, which impact coordination.

Visibility. Proximity, or physical co-location, is the primary means of
creating visibility in organizations (although see Wilson, O’Leary, Metiu and
Jett (2008) for an interesting analysis of perceived proximity). Through copresence (Mead, 1934; Goffman, 1963), organization members can see what
others working on the task are doing, get immediate evidence of the progress
of the work, and adjust their own work accordingly.
Monitoring. Visibility is created through formally established monitoring
practices as well as informal, lateral noticing of others’ behavior. For instance,
visibility is often used to coordinate activities in medicine: trauma room professionals are able to monitor tasks through their physical co-presence (Klein et al.,
2006); in research in other hospitals, clinical rounds allow for professionals to
update one another about a patient’s condition (Gittell, 2002). Such monitoring
is the norm for medical occupations, where senior members of the group oversee
the novices. This proximity allows experts to coordinate the work, intervene
when necessary, and make sure that treatment progresses appropriately.
In other types of workplaces, physical proximity creates lateral visibility to
coordinate the work. Whether performing surgery (Reagans, Argote, &
Brooks, 2005), planning NASA missions (Mark, 2002), or building radios
(Liang, Moreland, & Argote, 1995), group members’ visibility is enhanced by
their co-location, which allows them to easily keep track of what others are
doing. Hutchins (1991) also describes the importance of visibility for accountability in navigation teams: members utilize a “horizon of observation”—a
clear view of what others are doing—as they jointly pilot the ship. In these
cases, workers are not formally supervising one another, but can link their
own interdependent tasks by seeing others’ task performances.
Updating. Because visibility enables easy updating on task progress, in cases
where co-location is difficult, people attempt to make the work visible by
communicating the status of the work through other means. For instance,
creating visibility is more difficult in large-scale, geographically distributed
work such as software development (Metiu, 2006), and thus developers relied
on written notes and e-mails to account for the progress of the work. Bechky
(2006) points out that in the case of film production, where workers were
accustomed to coordinating through physical proximity, when they could not
be physically visible they updated one another frequently about their activities
via walkie-talkie. Even in cases where surgeons are co-located, when their view
of the work process is constrained, they sometimes adopt technological tools
that create visibility to account for what happens inside a patient as they work
together (Edmondson et al., 2001).
Familiarity. Proximity also leads to familiarity (Newcomb, 1956; Allport,
1954), which can enhance coordination. In the broader organizational

knowledge that individuals have of each other, an emotional element in the

knowledge and affect (Rockett & Okhuysen, 2002). In the literature on coordination familiarity is used more narrowly, focusing on task-related information

increased familiarity leads to stronger relationships, which encourages individuals to embrace their interdependence and allow them to more effectively coordinate (Gittell, 2002, p. 1410).
Anticipating and responding. Kraut, Fussell, Lerch and Espinosa (2008)

they can tailor their own actions to their activity partners. Such gains in

group as a store of information to perform the task (Hollingshead, 1998).

subsequently produce higher quality radios. Information about what others

the performance of work (Olivera, 2000). Examining an apparel and sporting

that a strong transactive memory system facilitates group members’ identification and use of relevant knowledge, enabling members to generate higher

Developing trust. Familiarity can also help coordinate through the impact it
has on the development of trust among individuals. The development of trust
is important in several ways. In the teams described by Liang et al. (1995)
members develop the confidence that others possess the radio-building expertise attributed to them. This trust allows individuals to expect that the target
person(s) can deliver a “technically competent role performance” (McEvily,

dyadic relationships. Each of these relationships involves “relationship specific
logics” that can only emerge as partners share private knowledge, and trust
enables and is formed through such sharing (p. 872). When individuals can
trust one another to perform their work in the proper manner, coordination is
enhanced.
Integrating Conditions for Coordination
As our review of coordination mechanisms shows, we have learned quite a bit
in recent years about how coordination is accomplished in organizations. The
five mechanisms for coordination—plans and rules, objects and representations, roles, routines, and proximity—encapsulate how emergent action
coordinates work in organizations. By using these five mechanisms for coordination as an organizing framework for our review, we begin to provide some
coherence to the findings from the current literature.
However, a reliance on mechanisms to describe coordination creates several
challenges for developing a theoretical framework for coordination in organizations. One problem is created by the way coordinative action is treated in the
literature: the interdisciplinary range of coordination research results in a variety of approaches to the object that is being coordinated. Crowston’s work
(Crowston, 1997; Malone & Crowston, 1990, 1994), for instance, approaches
coordination as a problem of managing the interdependence between tasks.
Thus, he focuses on elaborating the mechanisms that may coordinate tasks in
which resources are shared, or where tasks need to be decomposed into subtasks. In contrast, Gittell’s work examines relational coordination in groups
(2000, 2002, 2004). She is concerned with the quality of the relationships in
groups and she therefore measures how communication, shared goals and
knowledge, and mutual respect influence task performance. Faraj and Sproull’s
(2000) work is representative of the research on coordinating knowledge in
organizations. They demonstrate how recognizing the need for and location of
expertise, as well as the ability to bring that expertise to bear, improves team
performance. Because these scholars and others differ in how they explain
coordination in organizations, a comprehensive perspective remains elusive.

Another obstacle to comprehensive understanding of coordination is the
embeddedness of the findings in particular contexts. For instance, in
Okhuysen’s (2005) description of police SWAT teams, the development of routines is integrally tied to the regimented and paramilitary nature of the approach
used by officers. While a tight fit between the findings and the context is valuable
in establishing the relevance and effectiveness of these mechanisms, findings
such as these trade-off generalizability for accuracy (Weick, 1979). As a consequence, it is not immediately obvious how one would draw comparisons or
establish more general relationships among mechanisms, actions, and outcomes across studies. Further, the literature exhibits a remarkable array of terms
to describe similar mechanisms: procedures in one study resemble plans in
another, while in a third, a plan might be labeled a representation. In other
instances the difficulty is that a specific tool, such as a schedule, is sometimes
treated as a plan and at other times is treated as an object. While all of these
labels are appropriate for the contexts in which the mechanisms were studied,
this is problematic for an overarching conceptualization of coordination.
A third challenge lies in a lack of explication of the means by which
coordination happens: a focus on the “how” behind the mechanisms. In many
of the studies we surveyed, the explanation for why mechanisms worked to coordinate action, while present, was not front and center. In some studies, scholars
articulated this explanation but did not demonstrate it. For instance, in
Goodman and Leyden’s (1991) study of coal mining crews, familiarity among
crew members is inferred from archival records documenting previous experience working together. Their findings then show that those crews in which
individuals are more familiar, as measured through those work records, are also
more productive. Critically, in this research it is impossible to determine the
actual work practices or interactions among the miners; that is, we do not know
the details of why or how familiarity might enhance coordination in these crews.
In other studies, mechanisms are fully described and compared, but the
means by which they work to coordinate is not explored. For instance,
Crowston’s (1997) coordination theory describes alternative mechanisms for
managing the interfaces between tasks, actors, and resources. He argues that
many of these mechanisms are substitutes for one another; for instance,
markets and vertical integration are both ways to manage task assignment.
However, the theory does not explain why these might serve as substitutes:
what are the characteristics of these mechanisms that enable their successful
substitution? While Crowston’s (1997) typology is a useful one for understanding what mechanisms work in similar interdependent situations, it falls
short of a generalizable theory of coordination. Without making distinctions
in how mechanisms work, we cannot theorize about what they truly accomplish in the way of coordinating.

coordination mechanisms function. We began thinking about the possibility

Accountability
Accountability addresses the question of who is responsible for specific
elements of the task. By creating accountability, organization members make

accountability has been thought of as a way to enact formal authority and
organizational standards. For instance, reporting metrics hold people in an
organizational hierarchy to standards such as “on time arrivals” (Gittell, 2000)
or a given “time to fix the problem” (Orr, 1996). However, the use of accountability as an integrating condition in the coordination literature is broader in
both scope and means. By making responsibilities visible, parties become
accountable for their own contribution while also making other parties
accountable for theirs. Moreover, from this perspective accountability is not
only attained through hierarchical authority, as in the traditional coordination
literature, but can also be achieved in other ways, such as lateral interactions
in meetings or through public status reports. This highlights that accountability can be created through informal and emergent action as well as by formal

While accountability can sometimes be designed and built into the formal

formal means. Consider two different manufacturing plants: a standard hotplate assembly line (Hackman, Oldham, Janson, & Purdy, 1975) and a semiconductor equipment new product line (Bechky, 2003a). When building a

provides automatic accountability: the assembler who attaches the cable must
install it before the hot-plate reaches a second assembler, who is responsible
for installing the enclosure, and who can readily observe if the assembler up

and the task responsibility of both parties.

discussions around the prototypes that technicians spell out the responsibilities for assemblers. Thus, accountability in this setting is accomplished
through emergent dialogue, rather than a formal assembly structure.

through the schedule, departments and department heads can be held responsible for the activities they must complete during the production. This example also highlights that a critical element in the development of accountability
as an integrating condition for coordination is a reliance on trust: to the extent
that parties can count on a consistent and reliable performance of the work by
others (McEvily et al., 2003), establishing the scaffolding is sufficient to enable
coordination.

parties. For example, Symons et al. (1996) give one example of how routines,

our perspective, accountability is important because it enables integration by
allowing interdependent parties to ascertain the part that everyone plays in the
final production of the work.
Predictability
Predictability, the second integrating condition, enables interdependent
parties to anticipate subsequent task related activity by knowing what the
elements of the task are and when they happen. In other words, predictability
involves having a sense for what subtasks make up larger tasks and in what
sequence tasks will be performed. Being able to anticipate task related activity
allows parties to plan and perform their own work, and is essential for coordinated activity. As Simon (1945, p. 81) notes, planning and organizing are
useful not “merely to put each participant in the job he can best fill, but to
permit each to form accurate expectations as to what the others are going to
do”. Similarly, Rico, Sanchez-Manzanares, Gil and Gibson (2008, p. 164) note
that one of the critical elements of implicit coordination is the anticipation of
the actions and needs of others. Predictability allows people to fit their own
tasks into the whole through anticipation of when others will do their work.
Predictability can be developed and enhanced through both formal and
emergent mechanisms. For instance, consider the difficulties that a container
manufacturer has in coordinating activities between sales and manufacturing
(Lawrence & Lorsch, 1967). When determining production schedules, the
parties in this organization rely on the intervention of the formal hierarchy.
By escalating the scheduling of manufacturing operations to the highest levels
of the organization and awaiting their decision, the parties are assured not

that counterparties will abide by the solution imposed by management’s
authority, enhancing the predictability of the relationship and the process.
In contrast, consider the situation of police officers in crisis teams (Okhuysen, 2005) who must respond collectively and quickly to dangerous suspects
and uncertain situations. During rehearsals and briefing meetings, officers

types of situations. Thus, they rehearse fast-paced entries into locations until
the process is smooth and, before each mission, they discuss the approach
until there are no questions about each officer’s role. Over time, this familiarity with each others’ roles and responsibilities enables officers to confidently
anticipate what actions other members will take when faced with specific contingencies during a mission. Like the formal predictability engendered
through hierarchy in the container organization, a consequence of building
knowledge of one another through emergent interaction is an enhanced level
of predictability in the execution of the task (Reagans et al., 2005).
Predictability is built through familiarity because groups can organize their
tasks with knowledge of the people participating, using their partners’ preferences to guide the performance of the task. The performance of the task can
also be guided by plans and objects, mechanisms which help create predictability by defining the tasks that must be accomplished, as well as the timing
or order in which they must happen. For example, in hospitals plans are
enacted as medical protocols (Faraj & Xiao, 2006) that provide guidance for
the care that patients receive and establish the sequences in which patients
travel through different departments in the hospital depending on the
progress of their treatment. By providing specificity, plans and objects give a
more complete picture of task related activity.
By specifying sequences of tasks that must take place, routines can also
build predictability as an integrating condition for coordination. For example,
the activities in the housing office at a university, such as student move-in and
resident advisor selection, are guided by routines that take place every year
(Feldman, 2000). Because these tasks are a part of the routines of the housing
office, their execution can be expected by other interdependent parties. Even
if the details on how they are executed are not evident or clear to other departments, all parties know that these tasks will be completed every year.
As we noted, predictability allows interdependent parties to anticipate subsequent task related activity. Predictability is achieved through plans and
objects that define the tasks that must be completed; through familiarity that
allows for an understanding of others’ preferences around the work on the
task; and through routines that establish the tasks that must happen. When
high levels of predictability exist, individuals and groups can count on the
successful execution of the work of others and perform their own tasks
accordingly, enhancing integrating activities.

Common Understanding
Common understanding helps coordinate by providing a shared perspective
on the whole task and how individuals’ work fits within the whole. Common
understanding is high when participants in an interdependent activity share
knowledge of the work that is to be done, how it is to take place, and the goals
and objectives of the work. The literature considers common understanding
of three different varieties. One is a common understanding of the task in
terms of the specific “actions and strategies necessary to perform a task”
(Cannon-Bowers & Salas, 2001). Knowledge of the different parties in an
interdependent situation is also often described as having an effect on
coordination with one’s interaction partners (Reagans et al., 2005). Lastly,
knowledge about the broader context in which coordination activities take
place, such as knowledge of organizational goals, can keep everyone oriented
towards common task outcomes (Pinto et al., 1993).
Like accountability and predictability, common understanding can be
created both through formal, planned mechanisms and by emergent interactions in an organization. For instance, common understanding can be
embodied in assembly drawings, schedules, or other plans drawn up by the
organization. Thus, common understanding can be developed when plans
are created by senior managers and handed down a hierarchy to be implemented by those lower in the organization, as we noted earlier, and as Taylor
(1914) and Fayol (1949) might recommend. However, more recent work
suggests that common understanding can also be developed when plans are
the result of a bottom-up approach, in a more emergent fashion. For example, Bechky and Okhuysen (2009) show that schedules and location maps
built in both a SWAT team and film production crews by the members of
the groups themselves through an iterative process. By interacting to create
these plans, common understandings of how the mission or project will
unfold emerge, which orient and coordinate the work of the different
individuals.
As the previous examples suggest, plans and rules facilitate coordination by
creating common understandings of the whole task the organization is
pursuing, enabling the parties to orient their interdependent work. A similar
orientation to the whole task is provided by familiarity in organizations.
When people train together, they become familiar with the ways in which
their work fits together. By developing transactive memory systems, they generate a store of knowledge about how different tasks fit into the whole, and
who is likely to perform these tasks best (Austin, 2003). Routines act in a similar fashion, by bringing groups together and guiding the understanding of the
collective achievement of the whole task. The connections between tasks and
people that are embodied in the routine are what make the development of
common meaning possible (Feldman & Rafaeli, 2002).

Other mechanisms, such as objects and roles, are more closely linked to
creating understandings of particular ways to complete tasks. Because they
facilitate learning across groups, objects help groups translate their different
understandings tasks to create a common perspective. For instance, Bechky
(2003a) shows how engineers and assemblers use prototype machines to create an understanding of the production process. By demonstrating with prototypes, assemblers translate how they would build a part for the engineers,
who then have a better understanding of what is necessary when they return
to their design activities. Organizational roles such as boundary spanners
(Gittell, 2002) and brokers (Barley, 1996) coordinate similarly. By translating
the requirements of different groups, some technicians broker information
across groups (Barley, 1996), which allows for shared understanding of
particular tasks to spread.
While specific roles create common understanding through direct information sharing, roles also facilitate coordination by enabling substitution of
people when necessary to complete tasks. For instance, Bechky and Okhuysen
(2009) show how SWAT officers and film production crew members often
share extensive knowledge of tasks. Thus, when there is a problem, such as an
unexpected absence of a specialized camera operator, another camera operator can fill in so the task can be completed.
Common understanding coordinates through interdependent parties
developing a shared conception of the activities they are performing. Plans,
rules, routines and familiarity create common understanding at the level of
the whole interdependent task or work process. In contrast, objects and roles
are conducive to the development of fine-grained shared understandings of
how the work itself is performed. Common understanding is important as an
integrating condition because it enables participants to apply their effort
towards a jointly held conception of the work or of the process to complete
the work. Providing this common ground allows individuals and groups to
integrate their activities with those of other interdependent parties.
Relationships among Integrating Conditions of Coordination
Our presentation is organized to provide clear definitions and examples of
each of the integrating conditions for coordinated activity. However, a strict
differentiation of the integrating conditions is somewhat artificial, because as
people work interdependently, multiple options for coordinated action
emerge. In most situations where coordination is necessary, we see combinations of all three integrating conditions present simultaneously. Importantly,
though, work contexts differ with regard to which conditions are in the forefront of coordinating activities. By exploring how the integrating conditions—accountability, predictability, and common understanding—are
related to one another, we can tease out the tradeoffs and complementarities
among them.

One way in which the integrating conditions interact is by providing
support for each other. For example, one of the clearest relationships we noted
in the literature is that between predictability and accountability. In general,
we found that accountability supports predictability—making relationships,
interdependence, and task progress clear means that the parties involved in
the work can easily keep track of others’ activities, making them predictable,
and helping them deliver their own work in concert. The support between
accountability and predictability is perhaps most evident if we consider the
role of formal hierarchies in coordination, which define the responsibilities
and relationships between people and groups and, by creating this accountability, make the work more predictable across groups. Accountability and
common understanding can also support one another. Both Edmondson et al.
(2001) and Reagans et al. (2005) show that teams that have both high levels of
accountability and common understanding perform more effectively. In this
case, the two integrating conditions have a seemingly additive effect.
When we consider the relationship between predictability, the ease of
anticipating subsequent task activities, and common understanding, the
shared conception of those activities, we can see that integrating conditions
sometimes operate as substitutes for one another. One example of how this
works is provided by the use of shipping containers to transport goods as one
means of creating predictability. Through the use of containers port operators
reduce the ambiguity related to the types of equipment required and the
process of loading a ship, because all containers are of standard sizes. Such
standardization reduces the need to develop common understandings
between the ship captain, port operator, truck drivers, and customers—and
many elements of the shipping process can be taken for granted. When a
customer needs to transport a non-standard shipment, however, predictability
becomes problematic because common understanding (otherwise reflected in
standard size containers) is missing. For example, an auction house recently
shipped a large Koons sculpture from Dallas to London and faced coordination challenges due to the inability to use a standard shipping process
(“Splendor in the Grass”, 2008). Instead, due to the difficulties of transporting
the sculpture (including its size and weight and the desired arrival date), a custom container had to be built, a specialized airplane that opened in the nose
was chartered, and the shipment was accompanied across Europe by a police
escort. Obviously, the coordination of this shipment could not rely on the use
of standards as a mechanism to create predictability. The parties involved
instead had to actively develop common understandings about the process of
shipping such a unique item. This example shows that in some situations, the
relationship between these integrating conditions is one of substitution—
common understandings can replace predictability and vice versa. In general,
each of these examples shows how the integrating conditions that we propose
act in concert to enable coordination.

Discussion
The integrative conditions for coordination we propose are an alternative way
to understand the effects of the five coordination mechanisms. By describing
how the coordination mechanisms affect interdependent parties through the
creation of accountability, predictability, and common understanding, we
advance a conceptual perspective that captures both emergent and formal
processes of integration. In this section we summarize our integrative
perspective before developing the implications of adopting this perspective. In
particular, we discuss how an integrative perspective puts a focus on the work
being performed. We also explain the need to treat coordination as an ongoing accomplishment by examining the relationship between coordination and
other common organizational dynamics. Finally, we speculate on the challenges that new forms of organizing bring to processes of integration.
An Integrative Perspective on Coordination
Our review of the current literature allows us to see the importance that
processes of integration play in coordination activities. Using that as a starting
point, our perspective characterizing coordination in organizations goes
beyond the mechanisms that produce coordination to one that puts special
emphasis on the elements that enable integration. Coordination is enabled
when the interdependence among parties, their responsibilities, and the
progress on the task are all made visible through accountability. Additionally,
coordination relies on the ability of interdependent parties to anticipate
subsequent task related activity, that is, predictability. Finally, a shared
conception of activities and how they are performed, or common understanding, also enables coordination. Through coordination mechanisms that
produce these conditions, people succeed in integrating interdependent tasks
in organizations.
Critically, earlier work on coordination can also be viewed through this
lens, suggesting the broad applicability of our perspective. For example,
Fayol’s (1949) work on the design of managerial hierarchies within organizations can be seen as an effort to maximize accountability. By establishing
authority, reporting lines, and unity of command, the design of hierarchies
ensures that interdependencies and conflicts between different areas of the
organization can be managed and given resolution, exemplifying the power of
accountability for coordination. Similarly, Taylor’s (1914) scientific management, with its emphasis on standardizing work, tools, and parts is an extreme
instance of establishing predictability through material means. In this case
coordination is enabled by reducing ambiguity and specifying, in extensive
detail, how work is performed. Lastly, the establishment of Railroad Standard
Time in 1883 is a radical example of the development of common understanding. Originally conceived as a timekeeping system for the railroad system to

society, establishing one of the broadest forms of common understanding.
Our perspective on integration is also useful for framing the recent literature on coordination in organizations. As we have described previously,
recent studies have focused extensively on emergent action. Such action is
more readily understood by adopting an integrative perspective that can help
us understand interaction and interdependence within the context of the task.
Of course, the emphasis on emergent action is not completely new. In fact,
every study in the organizational design tradition contained an “informal
interaction” category, so we know that these scholars were aware of the
emergent characteristics of coordination. However, by getting closer to the
work and tasks in the organization, more recent work transforms emergent
coordination from an informal and marginal activity to one that is central to
explanations of coordination, providing a more complete and accurate picture
of the process. As a consequence, we believe that the framework we are proposing applies equally well to the settings studied in the past as it does to the
studies reviewed here.
Our work highlights the three integrating conditions that, working
together and separately, assist in the enactment of coordinated activity. The
adoption of the three integrating conditions as intermediate constructs
between coordination mechanisms and coordinated activity is helpful because
it provides us with an overarching framework that can explain many of the
findings of extant work, providing a richer and yet more parsimonious explanation. The use of the three integrating conditions provides a way to embrace
the wide variety of coordination mechanisms that have been identified in the
literature by explicitly focusing on what those mechanisms accomplish. In
other words, rather than relying on the presence or absence of a particular
mechanism to explain how coordination occurs, our framework forces us and
others to explicitly consider how people use partial mechanisms to perform
the work, enriching our explanations of coordination. Additionally, by focusing on the integrating conditions we grant an important theoretical position
to informal and emergent activities, which previously have been acknowledged only as the messy remainder of planned coordination. By explaining
how the integrating conditions can sometimes support one another and sometimes substitute for one another, our work also emphasizes the role of the
work context in determining how coordinated activity unfolds. The emphasis
on context also provides an explanation for the inherent limitations of the
grand plans of organizational design perspectives which are unable to articulate fully the requirements of the work.
Using an integrative framework to explore coordination forces attention to
the work as it is performed collectively. Moreover, a focus on integration is
also consistent with research exploring work in organizations which considers
how people interact with one another as well as how their work is situated in

result from this approach are strengthened by the incorporation of planned
and unplanned activities and their interactions.
Coordination as an Ongoing Accomplishment
By separating the integrating conditions from the coordination mechanisms
and the realization of coordination, we also highlight the ways in which coordination is an ongoing accomplishment in organizations. The integrating
conditions of coordination are necessary, but are not always sufficient for coordination to occur. Normal, everyday dynamics in organizations intrude on
coordination by eroding the integrating conditions. In these cases, the integrating conditions need to be reestablished for successful coordination to occur.
One example of a regular process in organizational life that impacts
coordination is turnover (Reagans et al., 2005). As individuals come and go in
organizations, coordination can suffer because both new and old members are
less knowledgeable about each other, directly affecting the familiarity that
enables predictability. In addition, new members also may not be as knowledgeable about the organization’s task—they may be specialists, rather than
generalists, for instance (Feldman, 2000)—which reduces common understanding. When turnover occurs, organization members have to find ways to
bring individuals “up to speed” on how to work together, for example through
training or socialization, so that the task can continue to advance. Alternatively, when turnover is an ordinary and expected occurrence, as in trauma
teams (Klein et al., 2006), arrangements that compensate for the expected
turnover, such as those that rely more strongly on formalized roles, can be
effective at overcoming this challenge.
Another fairly common occurrence in organizations that causes coordination to break down is a mistake in execution. When work is interdependent,
errors or mistakes create obstacles for people to accomplish their task. For
example, Bechky (2003b) shows how assemblers and technicians cannot rely
on the work progressing when mistakes occur in the assembly of semiconductor manufacturing equipment. If particular groups are mistake prone and they
are judged unreliable, then there is an erosion of trust and the predictability of

in the production processes that also affects accountability. When accountability is reduced, interdependent parties cannot easily perform their own
work, as they lose some of their ability to gauge the progress of the task.
Reestablishing that accountability through the active management of problem
resolution then allows coordinated activity to continue.
Another ordinary organizational dynamic that erodes the integrating conditions and cause coordination breakdowns are status differences across
groups, because high-status groups may feel no need to recognize the task
contributions of members of low-status groups. For instance, Metiu (2006)
shows how, in software production, high-status programmers refuse to read
the notes and comments that low-status programmers provide to document
the progress of the work. Because accountability requires an acknowledgement of mutual responsibilities, status differences that prevent such acknowledgement limit the development of accountability. Additionally, in situations
where large status differences cannot be overcome, members of groups may
not be able to trust one another. When working interdependently, low status
individuals in these situations will ask fewer questions and give less feedback,
for fear of offending others and the possible repercussions (Edmondson et al.,
2001; Lee, 1997, 2002). Such a situation leads to less sharing of knowledge,
limiting common understanding in the group.
As these examples suggest, coordination is under persistent attack by the
regular dynamics of organizations. What these examples show is that individuals and groups in organizations must constantly recreate the integrating conditions of coordination in order to jointly execute their work. This ongoing
accomplishment of coordination relies on emergent action creating the integrating conditions to bring collective and interdependent work together.
Additional research is needed to flesh out our understanding of how the integrating conditions and additional dynamic elements of organizations interact.
Directions for Future Research
Our examination of coordination has a very explicit emphasis on processes of
integration, derived from the extant literature. Although coordination also
involves processes such as specialization, recent work on coordination has had
a dominant focus on the challenges that integration presents. Such an emphasis on integration is not new. It is also evident, for example, in earlier work by
organizational design scholars (e.g., Lawrence & Lorsch, 1967). And while this
emphasis was lost in the years of empirical testing of organizational design
theory, which focused primarily on relationships between environments and
organizations (Padgett, 1992), the current wave of research on coordination
has returned to a concern with how integration takes place.
However, while much of the current work describes processes of integration in coordination, this emphasis is rarely made explicit. A focus on the

mechanisms of coordination has meant that the development of theory about
integration and integration processes is typically embedded in discussions of
how the mechanisms operate, rather than receiving attention on its own. Our
review, which highlights the conditions that are necessary for integration
activity to take place, forces us to separate the consideration of mechanisms
from the coordination they help achieve. This theoretical separation moves us
towards explaining the effect of these mechanisms on how work is performed,
and closer to the development of a theory of integration.
A more detailed and theoretically driven investigation of integration raises
important questions, particularly given the changing nature of organizations.
For example, new ways of organizing have changed both our understanding
of the impact of organizational boundaries as well as our conceptions of
work arrangements. “Boundaryless” organizations (Arthur, 1994; Arthur &
Rousseau, 1996) have shifted the nature and location of the task boundary. In
these organizations, some tasks are outsourced either to individual contractors or to firms. But, in the absence of centralized planning or hierarchical
structures, how do interdependent parties create accountability? The research
reviewed here suggests some possibilities of emergent forms of accountability
that could transcend organizational boundaries and hierarchies. For instance,
work that is outsourced to different organizations could be made accountable
through display practices such as the ones seen by Kellogg et al. (2006).
Additionally, because accountability cannot be created through hierarchy,
managers facing these integration challenges might consider emergent forms
of influence as an alternative (Kanter, 1989). Another approach could emphasize common understanding instead of accountability by transforming outsiders into insiders by sharing information about operational aspects or making
the project a joint endeavor (Hudson & Okhuysen, 2009).
Another boundary that organizations are blurring with increasing frequency is that between the provider and the customer. Here, the challenge is
that organizations would like their goods and services to be co-produced by
workers and customers to achieve higher levels of satisfaction and efficiency,
presumably increasing loyalty. Starbucks’ menu, with its “tall, grande, venti”
labels, McDonald’s value meals, and airlines’ self check-in kiosks all require
integration of the customer with the organization, such that customers
become co-producers. When the customer does not know or follow the script
set out by these organizations to create the service, though, they can disrupt
the work and impact the quality of service not just for themselves, but for
others also waiting in line. These organizations therefore face the need to
create common understanding with their customers, who would traditionally
be considered outsiders to the organization, in order to integrate the different
contributions into the task.

boundaries also create challenges for integration. For instance, the work of

that we have not done before while facing new task environments. We have

but clearly this is an area that is ripe for future research.
We began this review by noting the increased research activity and interest
in coordination over the past two decades. The resulting body of research has
expanded our understanding in useful ways. In particular, by approaching
coordination through a focus on work this new research explains how formal
and informal elements contribute to coordination. This perspective legitimizes
the informal elements of coordination that earlier research, such as that focused
on organizational design, set aside. The research on coordination in organizations also yields, through our examination, an integrative perspective on
coordination. This perspective is useful in deepening our understanding of
what happens when work is coordinated, and what it takes for the successful
execution of coordinated work. As new forms of work continue to emerge,
focusing on the integrating conditions of coordination will provide a robust
conceptualization to explain the activities in organizations pursuing these tasks.
