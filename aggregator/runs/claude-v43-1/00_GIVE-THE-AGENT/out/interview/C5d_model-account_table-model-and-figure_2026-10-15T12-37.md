# Interview brief, C5d: the model finished

Target length: under 800 words.

## What I did

I told the model as prose in ten passes, tested every cut against the 35 story rooms, and sent the account to three readers. Their returns fixed ten errors and 34 unclear sentences. One packet error was mine: I served too little of a room. The field analyst then read all 36 declarations against their members' quotes. I accepted 94 pieces of that advice and kept 30. With the readers' codes and the field analyst's, I recut Stigmergy by three codings under a rule I had logged first. Three later passes brought the model's own statements into line with the declarations. Following J's advice, one pass restored the room as the actor where members spoke for it. The last pass changed nothing. I then wrote the Gioia table (195 rows over five tiers), the figure (21 boxes, 20 arrows, each tagged with its proposition), and the implications, with ten expectations for the return.

## What I found

Rooms run one order: a start, calls for hands, building, keeping. Each step beats a null of shuffles within each room. What differs is what a room meets after it builds: neighbours, a rival, a loss, or nothing. With neighbours, members make agreements that no one can make every placer keep. Against a rival's attack call, the room's own members often refuse, and a refusal usually gives a reason. Decided order is rare (46 of 705 rooms). Members try to align without deciding how they are organized. Placement stopped at hour 73 for every room, and the room's work then turns to records and to deciding what to keep. The model explains how rooms organized better than whether their work survived. In the record, no form sorts kept from lost by much. The clearest difference is that rooms which organized more fall silent about the outcome less often.

## What surprised me

- **Stigmergy split.** The construct I had defended at the sources joined two kinds of record: reports of the work's state, and proposals of what to add. A second and third coder moved two of its three members out. I had written the rule that would move them, and I did not expect it to fire.
- **The strict reading drifted.** Three passes in a row each found more wording to remove. It took J's advice to see that the reading had demoted the room, the unit of the study, to single members. The rooms speak in the first person plural: "We have to shrink... We can't grow and maintain."
- **Border talk.** Where it came before any upkeep, it was mostly terms offered before any damage, not a complaint about damage.

## What I doubt

- **Stigmergy as a fourth-order theme of one member.** It breaks the stage C2 criterion. I kept it because withdrawing it would push the spine's building element out of its dimension. That is a judgment, and a reader may fairly call it a rename.
- **The thin arrows** (A05, A10, A14, A19) and the theorised template step (A11, A12). They stand, but on margins a different coding could close.
- **The bare answer's mechanism.** It comes from conversation analysis, and its reason test ran on attack calls only, not on calls to place.
- **The figure's left column is almost empty.** The model has few conditions: one judgment and one frame that claims no effect. I am not sure whether this is a fact about these rooms or about what talk in public records.

## Three questions I would most like to be asked

1. Is keeping Stigmergy as a one-member fourth-order theme defensible, or should it have been withdrawn and building and reporting left at the third order?
2. The model tells the rooms where the work decides whom they meet, and fails where a faith or a host's rule decides. Is that a boundary of the model, or a second structure the return should look for?
3. Since no form sorts kept from lost, is "which forms keep the work alive" the right question for talk in public, or should the return ask which forms keep the room talking about its work?
