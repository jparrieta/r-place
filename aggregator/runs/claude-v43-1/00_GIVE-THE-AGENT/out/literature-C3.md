# Literature, stage C3: the model read against the explanation shelf

This file holds what the literature did to the model, leg by leg. The model is stage C2's resting state, pass 5 (`model-p5.md`). The notes and the record of each step are in `notes-C3.md` and the diary.

**How claims about the literature are marked.** `verified`: I opened the work on the shelf in this session and quote it, with the page the extraction names. `recalled`: I am working from memory, with author, year and the claim I attribute, so a reader can check it. The read tier was served whole this leg: Puranam, Alexy and Reitzig (2014); Felin and Zenger (2016, the SSRN manuscript, pages the manuscript's); Felin and Zenger (2017); O'Mahony and Ferraro (2007); Bechky (2006); March (1991). Nothing on the on-request tier was served.

**What the read log can and cannot show.** `_mem_reads-C3.jsonl` and `_cite_reads-C3.jsonl` record what was served, not why. They cannot tell a check of a quote or a count from an abductive read I failed to log. Each abductive read of this stage is listed below with the diary line logged before it ran. The other room reads of this leg were checks of instances the model already cites.

## Leg 1 (C3a): work that contradicts the model

Four works contradict the model, or explain its phenomenon with something it did not use. For each: the two claims, what in the record decides between them, which of three things is true (the accounts disagree about the world, about the setting, or are about different things), and whose claim the disagreement bounds.

### O'Mahony and Ferraro (2007): a shared basis of authority (verified)

- **Their claim.** "Before an organization can develop a form of governance, it must establish a shared basis of authority" (p. 1081). In Debian, de facto founder rule gave way to a recalled leader, then a constitution, then elected leaders valued for organization building. They conclude that "coordinating their efforts and managing interdependence grew problematic without the help of people managing the organizational structure" (p. 1100).
- **The model's claim.** Rooms aligned their placers mostly without decided order (C01; F-006: Decided order is placed in 46 of 705 rooms).
- **What in the record decides.** Most rooms built and kept their work within hours with no shared basis of authority: "Help us draw Metroid over at /r/place!" [metroid/c0001, h4.27] was answered two minutes later with the location and a report of the placed piece [metroid/c0002, h4.29]. The paper made me look for where a basis of authority does form (abductive read 1, below). Decided order gathers in rooms placed on a colour-faction theme or on vetting guests' requests to build.
  - 7 of those 39 rooms carry it (0.179), against 39 of the 666 other rooms (0.059).
  - Held to room size by `items_in_window` quartile, the 7 stand against a null mean of 2.7 (95th percentile 5, p 0.014).
  - The gap lies in the two smaller quartiles (5 of 14 against 13 of 339). In the two larger it vanishes (2 of 25 against 26 of 327).
  - None of the 56 rooms whose members asked a host for space carries it.
  - The factions appoint officers and lay down rules: "Looking for positions to fill!" [PurplePixels/c0057, h8.58]; "Good idea! Would you like to be Recruitment Overseer?" [PurplePixels/c0137, h8.81]; "I am the new temp leader in the discord chat please don't give up we will not lose our corner" [TheGreatGreenBox/c0051, h14.64]; "Mods, BAN HIM" [orderofthebluecorner/c0009, h3.87].
- **Which of the three.** The accounts disagree about the setting. Debian integrates many contributions into one code base over 13 years. Most rooms made one picture in 73 hours, and the half-built picture showed the next placer what was missing. Where the canvas comes closest to their setting, a small body governing a territory for others, their pattern appears.
- **Whose claim it bounds.** Both. It bounds mine as a condition (rule 16), written into F-006's superseding line: decided order is rare in rooms whose work is their own picture, and gathers in the small canvas-born factions that govern a territory. It bounds theirs: organizing a single work in hours needs no shared basis of authority, so their "must" holds for governing a lasting body, not for organizing as such.

### Bechky (2006): role-based coordination in temporary organizations (verified)

- **Her claim.** Common portrayals of temporary organizations "as ephemeral and unstable are inaccurate: Temporary organizations are in fact organized around structured role systems whose nuances are negotiated in situ" (p. 3). The generalized role structure travels between projects by careers, and crews enact it by enthusiastic thanking, polite admonishing and role-oriented joking (pp. 8-13).
- **The model's claim.** Strangers coordinated by a start, calls and a visible half-built work. The model's two roles, the starter (R01) and the envoy (R02), are taken up on the canvas.
- **What in the record decides.** The roles the rooms name are coined there: "Hi, I'll be the ambassador for WIT on /r/place, we arent trying to write over you guys." [littlewitchacademia/c0049, h62.87]; "Good idea! Would you like to be Recruitment Overseer?" [PurplePixels/c0137, h8.81]. Her enactment practices appear without a role structure, and they enact a norm about the work, not a role. "Wipe out the Dolphins. They're building." [nyjets/c0086, h47.85] is answered one minute later by "Nah, keep /r/place neutral." [nyjets/c0087, h47.87]. The one role a room brings in is the host sub's moderator, whose acts are the host's line around canvas talk (P20), not the canvas work.
- **Which of the three.** The accounts disagree about the setting. She names it herself: "This prompts consideration of how temporary organizing can be successful in settings without a generalized role structure" (p. 17).
- **Whose claim it bounds.** Hers. The canvas lies outside her scope: no careers carry roles between rooms, and nothing makes the room a total institution. The model is one answer to her open question for this setting, and nothing in it changes.

### Felin and Zenger (2016; 2017): the theory-based view (verified)

- **Their claim.** Organizing starts from an actor's theory, "a set of contrarian beliefs" that frames which problems to solve (2017, p. 258). "Valuable theories require a contrarian belief" (2017, p. 262). The theory reveals "an architecture of problems to solve" (2016, p. 9). Organizations with a clear theory attract those who share it by self-selection (2017, p. 266), and "a key outcome of hierarchy is systematically advantaging one actor's theory of value over another" (2017, p. 265).
- **The model's claim.** A member's start gives calls something to gather around (A02). What the room makes is mostly given by its topic: its characters, its logo, its land's flag (P02). A design argued in a thread is settled by a template posted in it, not by authority (A11), and decided order is rare (C01).
- **What in the record decides.** On the order of events the record agrees with them: a start comes before calls in 64 of 89 rooms carrying both (A02). On what the start is, it mostly disagrees. Rooms build the emblem their members already share. The nearest instance of their theory reframes an existing mark: "On /r/place , the bottom left corner is green and labeled GREENCORNER . I say we make an OG logo and change it to GREENWALL. Anyone down?" [opticgaming/c0191, h6.43]. It is answered "We're all gonna have to come together to get it done." [opticgaming/c0192, h6.45]. An argued design is settled by a template, not a ruling: "Maybe make it 20x20? Or just a purple W?" [udub/c0008, h55.02], then "20x20 is possibly a better idea... Will update template image and upload here." [udub/c0009, h55.09].
- **Which of the three.** They are about different things. Their theory is a contrarian belief about economic value held against a market. A room's start is mostly a proposal of a picture its members already share. The model's names, a start and a proposal, are the right ones for the rooms.
- **Whose claim it bounds.** Neither is bounded by the record. The open part is whether each room inherited, negotiated, contested or changed what it made. That is stage C3c's question on the goal, and it is left there.

### Olson (1965): collective action without selective incentives (recalled)

- **The claim I recall.** I believe Olson (1965), *The Logic of Collective Action*, holds that a large group whose members share an interest will not act to secure it without coercion or selective incentives, because each member gains whether or not they contribute. Puranam, Alexy and Reitzig (2014, p. 171, verified) cite Olson (1971) for "privileged groups that value the public good highly enough to contribute regardless of the (non) contributions of others".
- **The model's claim.** Large rooms built and kept marks with no coercion and no selective incentive beyond credit and thanks.
- **What in the record decides.** Nothing here. The record holds who spoke, not who placed, so members who never contributed are invisible, and a claim about them is an absence claim, marked weak. It would be decided by placement counts per account against each room's readership. The study does not use those, since the canvas is not a declared ground (rule 7).
- **Which of the three.** Most likely the setting. A contribution costs one pixel per interval, and the cheapest answer to a call is one line. The contradiction is reported as open.

### Works read and not taken as contradictions

- **Puranam, Alexy and Reitzig (2014), verified.** The standing claim of the contract's section 1: a form of organizing is "a specific set of solutions to the four universal problems that any organization must address in order to exist" (p. 166). It also rules out "goalless networks, and crowds (but not mobs)" (p. 164). It is put to the structure at stage C3c, by design, and not decided twice. Read for this leg it resembles the model more than it contradicts it. The final product as "its own representation" simplifies coordination (p. 172). Authority in open source lies in the right to accept or reject contributions and membership (p. 173).
- **March (1991), verified.** No contradiction. Its organizational code, which newcomers learn and which learns from them, and its point that "the development of knowledge may depend on maintaining an influx of the naive and ignorant" (p. 86), bear on the residue of six pass 5 rooms (what a mark brings back into its room). That is stage C3b's material.
