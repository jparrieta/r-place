Abstract
This essay in honor of ASQ’s 70th volume surveys how technology-driven
changes in scholarly publishing have introduced algorithmic management to
organizational research. The internet greatly reduced the cost of publishing
journals and prompted an orders-of-magnitude increase in the number of
journals and articles while also foregrounding quantitative metrics for scholar-
ship. Given the academic incentive system of publish or perish, the new
online ecosystem has encouraged problematic practices by scholars and pub-
lishers that threaten the standards and values of organizational theory. The
advent of generative artificial intelligence within this milieu is almost certain
to worsen the publishing trends we have already experienced. Drawing on
prior literature about the centrality of deep intellectual engagement through
reading, writing, and interactions with colleagues, we propose a set of
reforms to preserve the sacredness of craft and community at the core of
our scholarly work.

Keywords: occupations/professions, technological change, generative AI,
philosophy of science, publishing industry, bibliometrics

customers after each task is completed, providing continuously updated perfor-
mance metrics (Cameron, 2024). Warehouse workers are guided by visual

entific work, as it has introduced entirely new tools to conduct, share, and eval-

fundamentally changed by these new technologies. Like other technologies
that shape research, generative AI will be adapted and implemented by schol-

of publish or perish, and a scholarly publishing industry driven in large part by
profit concerns. The immediate effects—greater efficiency in our day-to-day
research tasks—may seem benign, but the longer-term risks to our broad
enterprise and our ability to cultivate our skills as scholars are substantial.
    Although we know that knowledge workers resist some of the technologies
implemented at their workplaces (Kellogg, Orlikowski, and Yates, 2006; Pine

and Huising, 2023). And as knowledge workers adopt these technologies, both
their tasks and their expertise shift (Barley, 1986). The work of scientific
research is clearly subject to these forces. For instance, the use of MTurk and
other online study platforms has shifted the methods of researchers in psychol-
ogy: Scholars are now using fewer behavioral and more self-report measures in
their data collection as a result of using online respondents (Anderson et al.,
2019; Simon and Wilder, 2022).

    The burgeoning availability of these new digital technologies challenges cen-
tral pillars of organizational scholarship—our craft and our community. Our craft
is rooted in our engagement in the world and our apprenticeship with seasoned
experts, which connects us with the foundations of our knowledge and exper-
tise. These are not experiences that can be contracted out to technology. A
chatbot cannot substitute for the deep reading that sparks insights into the
social world. Our community is also rooted in concrete interactions in the semi-
nar room, the research meeting, the semi-anonymous review process, the edi-
torial board meeting, and discussions of personnel decisions. What is quality?
What is innovation? What counts as a contribution? All of these include deep
intellectual engagement as an essential ingredient. ‘‘Scholar’’ is a calling, not
just a job or a gig (March, 2018).
    Yet, deep intellectual engagement can increasingly seem like a lost art in the
current career and publication environment. In a system driven by quantitative
metrics, the point is not to be read but to be published and cited. Like unworn
articles of polyester clothing piling up by the acre in Chile’s Atacama Desert,
unread research articles pile up by the thousands in online open access jour-
nals.4 Within this context, the introduction of generative AI threatens not just to
manage our work by algorithms but to have algorithms do the work itself: litera-
ture searches, data collection, hypothesis generation and testing, and writing
and reviewing papers. AI augmentation to enhance research efficiency threat-
ens to become the algorithmic replacement of science.
    The complacency and even enthusiasm with which our field has met the rise
of these new technologies lead us to call for revitalizing scholarship in organiza-
tional theory. First, we briefly describe how the context of organizational schol-
arship has shifted due to technological changes to publishing, and we explore
the impact on the field and the practice of science. While we are in a moment
of technological flux and therefore cannot truly anticipate what generative AI
may become, we speculate on how generative AI will exacerbate the pressures
of efficiency and productivity. We offer some ideas for countering the potential
unwanted consequences for organizational theory, with a focus on craft and
community as the basis for the progress of science. We close with recommen-
dations on how the field—and journals in particular—can help preserve what is
sacred in our scholarship.


HOW INFORMATION TECHNOLOGIES SHAPE THE CONTEXT OF
PUBLICATION AND THE PRACTICE OF SCIENCE
Science is an inherently social activity, and journal publications are its essential
currency. From the birth of the first scientific journal in 1665 until today, words
in print, written and read by scientists, have been the conduits of knowledge
transmission (Davis, 2014). But scientific publications are not simply vehicles
for communicating findings among the scholarly community; they are also key
performance indicators for participants, used to evaluate individual scholars,
departments and schools, and journals. Our most venerable cliché is not

    The advent of the World Wide Web in the 1990s had a transformational
effect on scholarly publishing. Before then, journals were tangible physical arti-
facts that were costly to produce and distribute, as they had been since the
launch of the Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society centuries ago.
Libraries were stocked with shelves full of back issues, and academic disci-
plines often contained a limited number of elite journals to which members of
the field subscribed.5 The internet drastically lowered the cost of publication.
Consider the physical process of handling manuscripts before the internet: cre-
ating multiple photocopies of submissions, assigning reviewers via mail, com-
piling paper copies of reviews, copyediting text, laying out each issue, and
getting copies of the issue mailed to subscribers. Readers, in turn, may have
had to visit the library to photocopy articles from journals they did not receive.
By creating a means to process, review, and distribute scientific publications at
a much lower cost and faster pace, the internet revolutionized scholarly
publishing.
    Publishers and scholars adapted to this new technological environment by
drastically increasing the number of journals and articles in every scientific field,
including management. In 1999, the Web of Science included 61 journals in the
management category, which collectively published 5,901 articles. By 2022,
227 management journals published 27,693 articles. Beyond the 400 percent
increase in articles published in recognized journals, there are hundreds more
online journals outside the ambit of the Web of Science, with varying levels of
legitimacy, publishing tens of thousands of articles each year. Anyone can pub-
lish now; any piece of writing can find a home in an online journal.
    Freed from the constraints of physical space, publishers proliferated new
forms of review and publication. Particularly notable was the advent of open
access journals such as PLOS ONE from the nonprofit Public Library of
Science. Since 2004, PLOS ONE has published over 200,000 articles in 200
subject areas. The early idealistic goal of the open access movement, as exem-
plified by the PLOS journals, was to enable free and unrestricted global access
to research in perpetuity. Instead of relying on individual subscribers or libraries
to underwrite the expense of publication, open access journals charge authors
a fee (typically at least $2,000) to publish an article. This practice, favored by
some funding agencies, has now morphed into a munificent profit model for
publishers and has contributed to the explosion of journals and publications. As
Hanson et al. (2024: 1) noted, ‘‘Total articles indexed in Scopus and Web of
Science have grown exponentially in recent years; in 2022 the article total was
~47% higher than in 2016, which has outpaced the limited growth—if any—in
the number of practicing scientists.’’

potential. Moreover, the attendant massive growth in publication numbers has
made the literature more difficult to navigate and evaluate, leading to a host of
new scholarly metrics and measurement practices.


Dominant Measures: The Rise of the Journal Impact Factor and Google
Scholar Citations
By increasing the frequency and visibility of performance metrics, online publi-
cation has exacerbated problems in how scholars judge quality. Sociologists
have shown that quantification creates a compelling, outwardly simple means
of evaluating categories by standardizing different qualities into an easily com-
mensurable number (Espeland and Stevens, 1998; Espeland and Sauder, 2007;
Pardo-Guerra, 2022). Moreover, actors are reactive to these metrics, changing
their behavior to fall in line with what is being evaluated. And as we have seen,
when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Instead,
metrics amplify the pressures for efficiency and productivity in the field.
    Two metrics that have become increasingly central to the scientific enter-
prise are the journal impact factor (for journals) and Google Scholar citation
counts (for individual articles and scholars). The journal impact factor (JIF) has
become perhaps the dominant metric for publication quality. In the pre-internet
era, journal quality represented a shared understanding among professionals
and could be inferred by indicators such as the academic prestige of the editors
and board members. Members of the field might have an implicit list of A and
B journals, and judgments of quality were rooted in reading articles as well as
in informally assessing journal quality and reputation. The JIF was conceived in
1955 to help libraries decide which journals to subscribe to given their limited
shelf space, and in 1975 Thomson Reuters began publishing its Journal Citation
Report annually. The measure is simple enough, capturing how often the

    In addition to the challenges of evaluating journal quality, navigating the liter-
ature has become more complex for scholars, as the bounty of new journals
and articles vastly outstripped anyone’s ability to keep up. One enterprise was
uniquely experienced in scanning, categorizing, and presenting information to
online users: Google. Google Scholar was launched in 2004 by Anurag Acharya
and Alex Verstak in an effort to make it easier to locate scholarly articles online.
The service was an immediate success, and it grew in functionality over subse-
quent years. Crucially, in 2006 it added citation counts to its search results, and
in 2011 it created Google Scholar profiles for individual researchers, which
offered tools to assess the impact of individual academics through metrics
such as lifetime citation counts, the H-index, and other quantitative indicators
(Goldenfein and Griffin, 2022). Despite its pervasiveness in academia, Google
Scholar is very much a minor side-hustle for the Alphabet Corporation. Ten
years in, Google Scholar had just nine dedicated employees and no revenue
(Van Noorden, 2014). To this day, as far as we know, Google Scholar is a
revenue-free endeavor provided as a complimentary service to the scholarly
community.
    Google Scholar’s profiles and citation counts have become the standard
measure for individual evaluation in science in general and for organization the-
ory scholars in particular. Prior to this metric, citation measures were calculated
by the Social Science Citation Index on the basis of published articles, and they
were measured infrequently given the burden of paper journal publication. Few
scholars would take the trouble to recalculate their lifetime citations every quar-
ter when the latest print edition of the Social Science Citation Index arrived.
Some universities continue to use this measure, but it is rare, in our experi-
ence, to see a tenure evaluation in which Google Scholar plays no part. Career
concerns and the incentive system of academia inevitably drive scholars to
embrace the metrics used to evaluate their work, flattening the basis of com-
parison: A standard evaluative practice in some schools is to create spreadsheets
comparing candidates on the basis of A publications (perhaps augmented by
JIFs) and Google Scholar counts. Such practices can force commensurability
where it does not exist and drive out nuance and context in evaluations. Rising
scholars respond by prioritizing these metrics. As a testament to just how central
Google Scholar is to evaluations of one’s scholarly contributions today, consider
the eulogy for Michael C. Jensen, written in April 2024 by Eugene Fama, his
Nobel Prize–winning coauthor.8 Among the 11 brief paragraphs, Jensen’s Google
Scholar citations are mentioned in paragraphs 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7. Even beyond the
grave, one’s worth as a scholar is evidently best expressed by Google Scholar
counts.
    For academic literature, Google Scholar is analogous to Amazon. When a
shopper visits the Amazon site and searches for a particular product or cate-
gory, they are presented with a list of options in an order and format deter-
mined by Amazon’s algorithm. That ordering and format are contentious: They
are not based strictly on customer reviews, and critics claim that Amazon pre-
ferentially presents its house brands, or those brands that use its paid services.
Few shoppers make it past the second or third screen, so placement is essen-
tial to survive the competition within Amazon. Similarly, when researchers use
Google Scholar to search the literature, they are presented a list of articles in an

   How does Google Scholar rank documents? It seems no one knows for
sure. The site states, ‘‘Google Scholar aims to rank documents the way
researchers do, weighing the full text of each document, where it was pub-
lished, who it was written by, as well as how often and how recently it has

is private, our use of its output ‘‘means accepting an ex-ante evaluation of
scholarship premised on the invisible knowledge hierarchies built by Google,
that are entirely opaque, unknowable and unchallengeable’’ (Goldenfein and
Griffin, 2022: 19). Transparency is one of the core values of science. Relying on
inscrutable algorithms for any aspect of the scientific process violates these
values. This is also where the affordances of the technology meet the incentive
system of academia: Like vendors on Amazon, scholars seeking to enhance
their evaluations have incentives to game the system for advantage. It fits
Rahman’s (2021: 945) description of the ‘‘invisible cage’’ metaphor for algorith-
mic management: ‘‘a form of control in which the criteria for success and
changes to those criteria are unpredictable.’’ And the dangers of relying on a
single for-profit corporation for surveying and evaluating the literature are
immense. Imagine, for instance, if an authoritarian government hostile to
higher education came to exercise influence over the Google Scholar algorithm.
   The technological underpinnings of the publication and citation environment
have expanded opportunities for academic dishonesty. Since 2008, The
Scholarly Kitchen, operated by the Society for Scholarly Publishing, has hosted
a platform for exposing the problematic practices of journals and publishers and
has brought to light a large catalog of tech-enabled shortcuts that corrupt the
basic infrastructure of scholarly communication.10 For instance, journals and
their editors have become skilled at engineering JIFs through bogus citations.
One tactic is to edit special issues of other Web of Science–indexed journals
and ensure that authors heavily cite the editor’s home journal. Another is to
engage in citation cartels with one or more other journals to engineer mutual
citations. And editors have been forced to gain skills in search engine optimiza-
tion to ensure that Google Scholar and other web index services point to their
journal’s articles.
   Moreover, there are now entire industries devoted to helping scholars pub-
lish as much as they can afford. Scientists who are willing to engage in aca-
demic fraud have many opportunities. For-profit paper mills manufacture and
publish articles on their behalf, typically with fabricated data and results.
Auctions for authorship are also available online. And services openly advertise
to increase one’s Google Scholar citations. One vendor on the platform Fiverr
(pictured in Figure 1) offered to increase Google Scholar citations by 20 for
$100. Metrics of scholarly impact today are roughly as reliable as the reviews
on Amazon. This is the milieu in which generative AI has landed.

    Generative AI also offers a potential way to address the problem of expen-
sive and time-consuming data collection by generating synthetic data points to
augment or replace small or low-quality data sets, including data normally cre-

development is ‘‘automated social science,’’ in which generative AI creates
replicants to serve as subjects for ‘‘in silico’’ experiments that would be costly,
difficult, or impossible using actual human subjects (Manning, Zhu, and Horton,
2024: 1). The less-optimistic reality is that data fakery can now be automated
by chatbots. Whereas once it might have required artisanal care to change the
values in cells in a spreadsheet or to fabricate them entirely, generative AI
offers the option to fake data on a wholesale level.
    Naturally, reviewers have found that generative AI can save time by auto-
mating the review process; Liang et al. (2024: 1) reported that up to 17 percent
of reviews submitted for a 2024 AI conference could have been ‘‘substantially
modified’’ by LLMs. It seems only a matter of time before tenure reviewers
will display the same telltale signs of relying on generative AI, using words like
‘‘commendable,’’ ‘‘meticulous,’’ and ‘‘intricate.’’ Reading articles is hard and
time-consuming. With Google Scholar and ChatGPT, tenure reviews might be
completed in a half-hour rather than two days.
    While generative AI has generated huge excitement among some scholars,
it is not clear that these technologies can deliver on their promise. And even if
they improve and achieve their anticipated potential, we still need to think
about their ‘‘digital undertow’’ (Orlikowski and Scott, 2023: 5), the indirect and
unanticipated systemic consequences of their use. Inserting generative AI into

Organizational Science Within a Community of Scholars
What is our role as organizational theorists? The core of the academic enter-
prise in organizational theory is to develop and test knowledge about the work-
ings of organizations, or as ASQ’s masthead reads, we are ‘‘dedicated to
advancing the understanding of administration through empirical investigation
and theoretical analysis.’’ In doing so, we seek to advance the understanding
of organizations by building on and refining the knowledge of those who have
studied before us in ways that are true to what happens in the world of organiz-
ing. In other words, our role as scholars is to think deeply about organizations.
   Two intertwined dimensions of our work are vital for the continuation and
impact of organizational scholarship: craft and community. The roots of our
learning come from a commitment to craft—to doing our task well. In The
Sociological Imagination, Mills (1959) described the importance of intellectual
craftsmanship for sociology. He argued that we formulate the issues and trou-
bles that we notice in the world as problems of social science, and then we dig
deep into the data we can collect to figure out the answers. To do good social
science, then, means making research with our whole selves, continually think-
ing about research questions as we go about our lives, creating an expanding
set of ideas drawn from experience and reading, and constantly reflecting on
what we know and don’t know. Moreover, our craft is not just about ideas but
requires empirical examination supported by discipline and focus. Rigorously
generating a compelling research question and gathering and analyzing the
data that are appropriate to answer it take a lot of thought and engagement.
Moreover, to disseminate our knowledge effectively, we must fashion well-
argued papers that our colleagues want to read and can understand.
   These kinds of skills are developed through scholars’ concrete interactions
with problems of practice. As Sennett (2008: 9) described for all craft skills,
‘‘every good craftsman conducts a dialogue between concrete practices and
thinking; this dialogue evolves into sustaining habits, and these habits establish
a rhythm between problem solving and problem finding.’’ As he showed, there
is a relation between the hand and the head of a skilled worker, who practices
their craft in an imperfect world with incomplete tools and learns through
improvisation to fix the problems that arise. Similarly, Schön’s (1983) analysis
of how professionals think suggested that they learn through reflection in action
as they encounter practical problems. His analysis illustrated, for instance, how
architects’ skills develop as they think through how their building design and
the layout of their site intersect, and how a psychotherapy resident learns as
they sort through a patient’s explanations for feeling stuck.
   Craft is not only developed through concrete interaction with the world but
is also both tacit and relational, originating in social interaction with our more-
experienced colleagues. Engagement in a community of practice moves
apprentices toward competent practice in a field (Van Maanen and Barley,

1984). Novices learn by watching experts, doing smaller, low-risk tasks, and
gradually performing the tasks that are more central to an occupational group
(Lave and Wenger-Trayner, 2011). Schön’s architects and therapists were not
reflecting alone on their problems of practice but were asking their expert col-
leagues for guidance and ideas. Similarly, novice organizational theorists learn
in an apprenticeship model through interaction with faculty. And the commu-
nity of organizational scholars sets the criteria for what good research is,
together defining the standards of our craft.
   This engagement suggests that it is important to be attentive to craft and
community as we think about how AI tools might affect our research process.
Hand and head need to be actively involved in order to become an organiza-
tional theory craftsperson. Take just one step in this process, the literature
review, and consider how the adoption of ChatGPT or other AI tools would
influence it. We are not aware of systematic surveys on its use, but our anec-
dotal experience suggests that Google Scholar is widely—if not universally—
used currently as the search tool for reviewing articles in a particular domain.
Ask a doctoral student in organizational studies how they access the literature,
and you are almost certain to hear that literature searches begin with Google
Scholar (and, implicitly, that anything that does not turn up on the first three
pages of results is likely to be ignored). Our collective understanding of what
the literature says about, for instance, prospect theory or absorptive capacity
therefore relies heavily on an inscrutable algorithm that is not open to inspec-
tion. This means there is already a lack of transparency about what is suggested
as the relevant articles for their inquiry. Adding ChatGPT and NotebookLM fur-
ther complicates the literature review problem: Additional anecdotal evidence
suggests that some students rely on ChatGPT to summarize their required read-
ing for their doctoral classes. So not only might students be reading the wrong
articles, but they might not be reading the articles at all!
   Asking generative AI to summarize arguments or generate questions about
an article bypasses the hands-on engagement that is required for the develop-
ment of quality craftsmanship in organizational theory. Craftsmanship means
both digging thoughtfully into the theoretical ideas of our scholarly forebears
and getting a grasp on the empirical problems of the world. This kind of intel-
lectual engagement entails deep reading and reflective writing in the context of
repeated interactions with colleagues.
   Scholars of literacy and neuroscience show that deep reading (reading in a
focused, deliberative way) engages a host of analytical processes—analogy,
inference, engagement with background knowledge, and interpretation—that
lead to bursts of new thought (Wolf, 2008, 2018). Wolf wrote that it is the time
we invest in deep reading that enables us to engage the inferential and critical
analytic functions of our brains in order to transform information into knowl-
edge that will take hold in our memories. In deep reading, we connect our own
background knowledge with new information: Our brain creates analogies and
hypotheses in which we compare what we read with what we already know,
and we use evoked feelings and senses to understand the author’s perspec-
tive. Quick skimming does not accomplish this transformation of knowledge,
as ‘‘the discernment of the truth and value of new information depend on this
allocation of time’’ (Wolf, 2018: 192). Without deep reading and the creative
thinking to reflect on what is read, students will not learn to be critical about
organizational scholarship or to develop novel ideas.

   Writing serves a similar function in the development of scholarly ideas: We
think as we write (Bazerman, 1988; Martin, 1994; Ong, 2012). Moreover, writ-
ing is critical to the functioning of scientific communities (Fayard and Metiu,
2014). In the writing process, scholars must make their ideas concrete and
clear by articulating them on the page, adapting them to address their imagined
audience. Writing encourages the detailed specifying of ideas in order for read-
ers to engage with and understand what is written, and it allows both readers
and writers to reflect on those ideas. Through these mechanisms, scholars
build relationships, share concrete knowledge, and promote the development
of their invisible college (Price, 1963; Crane, 1972).
   Moreover, literature reviews serve multiple purposes. When we ask a doc-
toral student to do a literature review, the hope is that they will form interpreta-
tions, separate facts from speculation, synthesize the ideas they are reading,
and be inspired to develop their own ideas. Done with advisors or in a class,
this is an important practice for our community to structure engagement not
only with prior knowledge in our field but with colleagues. Our connections with
our colleagues, junior and senior, teacher and learner, are vital to the develop-
ment of ideas. In his analysis of the English author E.M. Forster’s aphorism
‘‘How do I know what I think till I see what I say?’’ Swedberg (2020) suggested
that this combination of reading, writing, and interacting enhances theorizing
and generates clear and creative scientific thought.
   Scholars are using generative AI not only to write reference letters and
reviews but to write papers. Clearly, the scholars publishing articles with ves-
tiges of ChatGPT-speak remaining in the text are not writing reflectively, nor
are reviewers and editors deeply engaging in reading their manuscripts. We are
fortunate to be in an occupation in which our job is to think, yet some are will-
ing to cede that responsibility to a technology. Instead of rolling our eyes at
scholarly ChatGPT memes, we need to take the introduction of this technology
seriously and respond.

PRESERVING CRAFT AND COMMUNITY IN A WORLD OF GENERATIVE AI:
REVITALIZING ORGANIZATIONAL SCHOLARSHIP
The current publication ecosystem gives reason to worry that generative AI will
not be implemented to improve our work and deepen our insights. Instead, it
may be used to automate many of our essential research practices in the ser-
vice of efficiency. The artifacts of science—publications and metrics—can feel
as if they live in a disembodied sphere apart from the thinking work of scholar-
ship. But organizational scholarship is an inherently social activity, with deep
reading and reflective writing at its core. We do not seek just the accumulation
of facts but the evolution of ideas and interpretations. Therefore, we worry
about the loss of craft and community. Most fundamentally, we worry about
the abandonment of deep reading. Intellectual engagement is at risk.
   In response to concerns about the corporatization of the university and the
acceleration of research culture, some have suggested that we embrace slow
science (Berg and Seeber, 2016; Stengers, 2018). Scholars have begun to
investigate the impact of massive increases in production rates on science, and
they have found that while the quantitative growth in articles is enormous, this
growth is not beneficial to the progress of scientific fields. The increase actually
has narrowed the range of citations in fields to older, more-canonical work (Chu

and Evans, 2021) and has produced a smaller percentage of disruptive articles
(Park, Leahey, and Funk, 2023). In other words, as production rates increase,
scientists are building more narrowly on what is already known rather than pub-
lishing novel work. Given that massive increases in publication rates are not
improving science, we believe there is value in slowing down to think more
deeply.
   Revitalizing organizational science aims to maintain deep reading and not
metrics as the core of our research and evaluative processes. In advocating for
bringing our intellectual process back to life, we propose two types of interven-
tions to preserve and enhance organizational scholarship in these changing
technological times. One focuses on maintaining craft through the apprentice-
ship model, and the other focuses on the role of the community.

For Craft
Reinforcing deep intellectual engagement as central to our craft can be done
in several ways. Through the more formal channels of doctoral training, for
instance, we already have mechanisms to encourage deep reading as a means
of understanding organization theory. First, in our doctoral classrooms, we
should continue to promote the value of depth of knowledge in the field. Many
of us have memories of seminars from our own doctoral training, in particular
the type presided over by the brilliant and revered scholar with sky-high expec-
tations of the discussion. Students never dreamed of arriving unprepared for
those seminars: You had to read closely and carefully if you did not wish to be
humiliated in class. Maintaining high standards in the seminar room will push
our students to learn the craft of reading and to make cogent arguments rele-
vant to the literature in the field. By teaching students what a thorough litera-
ture search looks like, encouraging them to use the literature to write carefully
when we assign them a paper, and not allowing them to hand in summaries
written by generative AI, we ensure that our standards for the craft remain
high.
   Similarly, while serving on qualifying examinations and dissertation commit-
tees, scholars should continue to model and encourage a deep knowledge of
the field through reading. This deep knowledge relies on diving into original
research rather than skimming and citing review articles. On qualifying exami-
nations, scholars might consider requiring students to create a list of what they
think are the 100 most influential articles in their area. Then, read the list, share
opinions, and discuss them further together. Asking questions on these exams
that require students to put important literatures in conversation with each
other, rather than merely summarizing an area, also encourages deep reading
and thinking through writing.
   Additionally, it is up to faculty to informally model deep intellectual engage-
ment in offices and hallways. Continue to keep books in your office, and
encourage your students to read them. Scholars can let students and junior col-
leagues know how much time it takes to stay on top of the literature and can
talk about the intense work required to write a paper. Making clear how much
effort it takes to actually write a cogent, meaningful argument and create an
important contribution to the literature focuses attention on slow and thought-
ful intellectual processes rather than quick publication shortcuts. In addition to
success stories, scholars could share details from their ‘‘shadow CV’’ (Berg and

Seeger, 2016: 65): the stories of disappointment about their less successful
papers—not just the ones that were stuck in a drawer because the empirics did
not work out but the mediocre articles that were published despite sloppy argu-

about how problems can be solved is a foundational mechanism for developing
professional expertise (Schön, 1983; Lave and Wenger-Trayner, 2011).
    Obviously, none of these suggestions strays far from the traditional role of
scholars in an occupational community helping novices develop their craft.
They are merely a reminder to reinforce these values with students at every
opportunity as they face the changing environment of scholarship. However, it
is also important to explicitly address the question of how to treat generative
AI in the research process.
    Discussions in our research community about generative AI sometimes take
the tone of a fast train to academic success: Just hop aboard, stop wasting
time, and your career is guaranteed. However, it behooves us to think about
the risks of jumping on a train that may be traveling in the wrong direction.
Generative AI presents itself as an authoritative collaborator (Messeri and
Crockett, 2024), but is it a trustworthy one? Scholars should consider not just
the efficiency of AI technologies but their effectiveness and the deeper implica-
tions of their use.
    We know that experts take care to look under the hood of the tools they
adopt (Bailey and Barley, 2011; Anthony, Bechky, and Fayard, 2023). Rather
than immediately trusting the outputs of a new tool, experts first ensure that
they are accurate and effective at supporting their work. The appearance of
authoritativeness presented by generative AI is a projection, and scholars
should treat it as such. Research demonstrates across many implementations
of AI in the workplace that it glosses over the seams and rough patches in its
output. And these issues must be repaired by the people who understand the
organizational context (Anthony, Bechky, and Fayard, 2023). For instance, faced
with a shallow literature review from a doctoral student, one of us suggested a
list of more-useful articles on the subject, turned to the other students in the
room, and asked them to also offer reading ideas to the author. Together we
came up with an augmented list of articles for the student to start from, and
tracing backward and forward citations from those articles led to a more thor-
ough review of the ideas in this literature. Repairing the insufficient output from
the technology not only helped the student learn but also built more extensive
knowledge and shared values within the community.
    Moreover, scholars should question the deeper implications of using genera-
tive AI. We need to take the time to investigate the outputs of generative AI
thoroughly, using them as a starting point rather than assuming they have
knowledge equivalent to a well-trained and trustworthy colleague. Even more
important, it is up to the research community to articulate a relationship with
these new technologies. How do responsible researchers bring generative AI
into their craft? What does it mean for the future of the field if we encourage
the use of AI for supposedly mundane tasks? In radiology, for instance, a field
with overwhelming exposure to machine-learning tools, professionals are wor-
ried that offloading easy scans to AI will result in novice radiologists lacking the
grounding in essential tasks that enables their senior colleagues to do more-
challenging work (Byju, 2024). How can our field avoid the potential dumbing
down of future scholars if we adopt generative AI wholesale instead of shaping

For Community
The community of scholars has several tools available to center the practice of
deep intellectual engagement. Two domains are especially important in reinfor-
cing community standards: the tenure review process and the journal review
process.
   First, it is widely lamented that hiring and faculty evaluation processes are
heavily based on metrics—at the crudest level, counts of articles and citations.
This reinforces the algorithmic management of science and undermines the
practice of deep intellectual engagement. Consider if the tenure review process
allowed candidates to be evaluated on just their very best articles as defined by
the candidates. Reviewers would be expected to read each piece carefully and
reflect that in their letters of evaluation. Promotion and tenure committees can
add a layer of evaluation of the quality of external letters, giving greater weight
to letters that reflect greater engagement with the articles. Indeed, some
schools have already implemented practices of evaluating external review let-
ters, requiring faculty to grade the quality and thoughtfulness of these letters.
The widespread adoption of such a practice could help to address the evident
decline in deep reading seen in external letters in recent years: If letters count
only to the extent that they reflect thoughtful engagement, and not just a quick
scan of Google Scholar, we might elevate our most vital moment of evaluation
and save it from algorithmic management.
   Some institutions have sought to de-emphasize metrics in faculty evalua-
tions and to center the question, ‘‘What did this scientist do, and why does it
matter?’’ The former dean of a biological sciences unit stated, ‘‘We decided to
ask applicants to tell us what they considered to be their three most important
publications and why, and to submit a copy of each. We asked simple, direct
questions: what have you discovered? Why is it important? What have you
done about your discovery? . . . The three publications chosen by the applicant
told me a lot about their achievements and judgement. Often, they highlighted
unconventional impacts of their work’’ (Benedictus, Miedema, and Ferguson,
2016: 455). This method of evaluation would help to revitalize our practice and
de-emphasize the metrics favored by algorithms.
   Second, journals are a central site for organizing scholarly communities, and
more pointedly, for resistance to the anti-intellectual currents of for-profit pub-
lishing corporations that led us to this dilemma. Ideally, journals embody the
idea of a community of scholars, with editors and reviewers engaged through
deep reading and vigorous debate about standards. Journals such as ASQ
make their high-quality review process a point of pride and select board mem-
bers and editors based on the quality of their readings and critique.13 At their
best, journals are a lively home for scholars committed to a common cause and
are open to the evolution of norms and standards of quality. At their worst,
journals are impersonal venues engaged in posting electronic articles online for
profit, putting their imprimatur on work of questionable quality that is motivated

reviewing organized? What percentage of reviewers who are asked agree to
write a review for the journal? Who gets on the editorial board and how? Which
practices ensure the rigor of the review process? How selective is the journal?

informative than that of the JIF. And optimistically, such rankings could inform
other influential lists such as the FT50 or the UT Dallas journal list. For instance,
imagine if admission to the FT50, a highly lucrative distinction, required journals
to document the quality and rigor of their review process, their adherence to
standards of open science, and the public benefit of the work they publish.
   Moreover, the ease and low cost of starting a new journal today have given
editors and boards a weapon with which to respond to publishers that prioritize
profit over quality: quit en masse and perhaps start a new journal. This has
occurred multiple times over the past several years. Over 400 editors, board
members, and authors associated with the Wiley journal Gender, Work and
Organizations sent a letter resigning from the journal in response to Wiley’s
heavy-handed efforts at policing the journal’s contents.14 The letter stated, ‘‘We
no longer identify with the aims, scope or undemocratic practices and pro-
cesses underpinning the journal, and we have no trust that the current govern-
ance is aligned with the values of our inclusive, feminist community,’’ and
claimed that Wiley was ‘‘sabotaging world-class scholarship.’’ The editorial
board of Theory and Society also collectively resigned in response to grievances
against publisher Springer Nature and, a few months later, reassembled as a
new journal titled Theory and Social Inquiry.15
   Journals and their constituents—we scholars—have tools to resist the
encroachment of profit-driven technologies and to maintain what is sacred
about our work. We should use them.

eration, the internet drastically lowered the cost of publishing and thus greatly
increased the number of publications. Quantitative metrics of impact became
increasingly central to the publication game, pushing the field toward a form of

scholarship, from literature review to data analysis to the writing and reviewing

can learn from the methods of resistance used by other professional commu-

and the practice of deep reading. We welcome the dialogue to follow.
