       The unit of observation in most population ecology studies is the
individual organization (see the review by Carroll, 1984). Most recent
empirical analyses follow the life histories of specific organizations,
studying events such as birth, death, and merger. In what sense do these
studies address questions at the population level?
       In the case of organizational mortality, which social scientists want
to understand for various practical and theoretical reasons (see the re-
view by Carroll, 1983), the approach is to collect life histories on sam-
ples (or whole populations) of organizations and estimate the effects of
various covariates on death rates. Researchers tend to find, for example,
that death rates of many kinds of organizations decline sharply with age.
The reasons advanced to explain this typically include such causes as
collective learning or development of trust within work teams (see
Stinchcombe, 1965).
       Recent analyses of mortality processes from a population ecology
perspective also specify dependence of mortality on age and use char-
acteristics of organizations as covariates. It might seem that the unit of
analysis is really the individual organization. Admittedly population
ecologists seek to understand demographic regularities in the rates at
which vital events occur to organizations. Yet their interest does not lie
in explaining organizational mortalityper se. As we explained elsewhere
(Hannan and Freeman, 1977; 1984; Hannan, forthcoming), population
ecologists want to understand the dynamics of organizational diversity,
how social changes affect the mix of organizations in society. But anal-
yses of mortality patterns in populations provide only one element of
an explanation of the dynamics of diversity. Information about mortality
patterns, then, must be combined with similar information about other
vital rates in order to make inferences about diversity.

organization. It is a propertT of a population or a community of orga-
nizations. In seeking to explain the dynamics of diversity, population
ecologists develop propositions about processes at the population level.
It is in this sense that the population is the unit of analysis.

DISCONTINUITIES IN THE WORLD OF ORGANIZATIONS

      Success in using the above theoretical strategy requires that mean-
ingful (non-arbitrary) populations of organizations be identified. Since
populations are defined in terms of organizational forms, this amounts
to saying that meaningful forms must be distinguished. Meaningful pop-
ulations can be most securely identified w h e n the w o r l d of organiza-
tions is discontinuous and there is reason to think that a partition of
organizations into populations reflects discontinuities.
      Conventional cognitive maps of the social world, as reflected in
language, do make numerous, sharp distinctions among organizational
forms. We routinely distinguish hospitals, prisons, political parties, uni-
versities, stock exchanges, coal mines, and fast-food chains. Within each
category`, finer distinctions are also routinely made.

research, as in recent studies on collections of voluntary organizations

and pairwise relations be supplemented by others that emphasize the
dynamics of boundaries in organizational space. It is instructive first to
consider a seemingly quite different problem. Social life in many soci-
eties is channeled by boundaries that partition populations into ethnic
groups. Such boundaries persist in physical and social space despite large-
scale movement of individuals across them. How is the persistence of
ethnic boundaries to be explained? One approach, which parallels those
sketched in the previous section, looks to differences in socio-cultural

Instead of beginning with problems of classification, this approach be-

       Most important in considering boundaries around forms are seg-

cases, on the initial conditions). If segregating processes dominate, dis-
continuities are sharp and distinctions among forms reflect real quali-
tative differences, tf blending processes dominate, distinctions among
forms are less meaningful.
       Some of the characteristics of organizations cited in dependency-

technologies and the transaction costs involved in implementing them
play such a role. But most of the discussion of this section attends to
factors that have been given little weight in previous treatments: social
networks, collective action by members of organizational populations,
and, particularly', institutional processes.

       The case of the semiconductor industry is instructive here. It is
widely assumed that the firms producing semiconductor devices (and
integrated circuits) constitute an industry. That most such firms have
located in the Santa Clara Valley, now dubbed Silicon Valley, has doubt-
less encouraged this view of a bounded industry with a distinctive tech-
nology, distinctive organizational forms, and closed networks of mobil-
ity. Is this industry indeed sharply differentiated from the broader
electronics industry?
       The distinction between semiconductor producers and other elec-
tronics manufacturers does seem to be based on real differences in tech-
nology. As Brittain and Freeman (1980) noted, the dominant producers
of vacuum tubes failed miserably in trying to produce semiconductor
devices. This failure apparently stemmed from discontinuities between
the two technologies. Organizational structures and management styles
appropriate for producing vacuum tubes--i.e., standardized methods of
production and tight managerial c o n t r o l s - - c o u l d not accommodate the
work arrangements demanded by the new products, which involved high
levels of uncertainty and rapid technical change. Engineers with spe-
cialized knowledge of semiconductor design and fabrication eventually
left the giant electronics firms to join or start semiconductor firms using
different organizational forms.
       Williamson (1975, 1981 )has argued that technological differences
per se are less important in determining organizational forms than qual-
itative differences in transaction costs. Technology surely affects the
costs of production and of completing transactions, but so do control,
scheduling, supervision, enforcement of contracts, and so forth. Efforts
to minimize transaction costs typically result in bundling sets of trans-
actions together within the same corporate actor, and Williamson calls
the result of these processes the "efficient boundaries" of the organi-
zation. When transaction-cost considerations lead to distinctive and per-
sistent bundling of a set of transactions, an organizational form will tend
to arise. Thus discontinuities develop among populations of organiza-
tions engaged in difterent kinds of transactions.
       Closure of social n e t w o r k s also gives rise to distinct forms. For
example, when a set of organizations hires each other's employees, it
develops a high degree of inbreeding with respect to the elements of
structure coded in individual m e m o r y and routines. Inbred populations
tend to diverge from other populations of organizations. Idiosyncratic
language and culture develop and diffuse through the closed population,
sharpening the differences between insiders and outsiders. Because such
language and culture tend to become markers of competence, inbreed-
ing feeds on itself, and populations thus separated in such networks tend
to become different social worlds.

kinds of institutional processes. One kind is purely structural: a social
actor (using a specific organizational f o r m ) is institutionalized to the
extent that other powerful actors in the system endorse its claims in
disputes (Stinchcombe, 1968). In this sense, labor unions b e c a m e in-
stitutionalized in the United States only after passage of the Wagner Ac~
in 1935 which held that organizations using a specified "labor union"
form had special standing in making certain claims. That is, the state
committed itself to intervene in support of workers' claims if they used
this organizational form (but no other).
       The case of labor unions illustrates an important point. The events
that institutionalize a boundary around an organizational popula-
tion often simultaneously codify the features of the form. Both insti-
tutionalization and the accompanying codification strengthen bound-
aries around forms.
       Institutionalization conveys powerful advantages. Because institu-
tionalized actors can call upon other powerful actors for aid in resisting
raids on their resources, institutionalization lowers mortality rates. Un-
less this tendency is offset by a birth process that blurs boundaries, such
a process produces a world of organizations with clear discontinuities
and defended boundaries. That is, selection in favor of institutionalized
forms intensifies the boundaries around the forms that emerge from the
selection process (Hannan and Freeman, 1984).
       A second kind of institutionalization occurs w h e n it remains un-
questioned that a certain form is the "natural" way to effect some kind
of collective action (Meyer, 1983). The labor union form b e c a m e in-
stitutionalized in this sense long before the Wagner Act, for several de-
cades earlier workers had stopped debating w h e t h e r labor unions w e r e
the natural vehicle for collective action for improving conditions of work.

often retained the craft principle of organizing by work role rather than
by industrial location, they incorporated incrcasingly diverse kinds of
workers, and this had an impact on structures of organization.

the current joint ventures between Japanese and American auto makers
and between French and American elite wineries may- result in orga-

BOUNDARY DYNAMICS AND DIVERSITY

       Segregating and blending processes have immediate implications
for organizational diversity, which is a main concern of ecological the-
ories (Hannah, forthcoming). When segregating processes dominate, in-
stitutional and other constraints create holes in the social s t r u c t u r e - -
that is, some feasible kinds of organizations do not c o m e into existence
because of these constraints. Thus it appears that ( 1 ) strong segregating
processes limit diversity, and ( 2 ) elimination of boundaries necessarily
increases diversity of forms. There are surely historically important cases
in which this was so. As Marx and others insisted, destruction of feudal
constraints on production organization (especially constraints on real-
location of land and labor) was essential to the emergence of capitalism
and the accompanying proliferation of forms. Moreover, Stinchcombe's
(1965) broad account of the importance of institutional and resource
constraints on the creation of new kinds of organizations reinforces and
clarifies the Marxian view.
       We think the relationship b e t w e e n segregating processes and di-
versity is more complicated than has b e e n noted previously, however.
The existence of holes in the social structure may for instance actually
increase organizational diversity in some cases. Consider processes of
competitive exclusion, the tendency for one population to eliminate all
others that depend on the same set of resources (Hannan and Freeman,
1977). The existence of strong boundaries presumably localizes corn-

to build organizations. Reduction of constraints was apparently m o r e
than balanced by increased variety of resources. The key question in
exploring the effects of discontinuities on diversity, in the organizational
world is w h e t h e r the creation or elimination of a boundary increases
or decreases the n u m b e r of distinct resources and constraints. For any
concrete historical case, answering this question demands close analysis
of the boundaries and the processes underlying them. The perspective
sketched in the previous section does not answer such questions, but
it does provide a framework for addressing them.

to focusing on boundaries has numerous implications for empirical re-
search. Perhaps the most obvious, and surely the most important, is that
boundary processes should be studied directly. Each segregating and
blending process discussed above is amenable to empirical analysis with

In other words, answering such questions means among other things
studying empirically where organizational forms come from.
       Our perspective on boundaries also has implications for defining
forms for empirical analysis of ecological hypotheses. As stated above,
identification of boundaries provides a provisional map of the organi-

analysis involves, first, using information on segregating and blending

provisional set of boundaries identifies discontinuities. There are at least

regions in the distributions. The second is to use the partitions implied

analysis to see whether the distinctions are reflected in estimates of the

distinction between craft and industrial unions produces a partition of
the population of unions with stronger competitive interactions within
populations (that is, within the populations of craft and industrial unions)

this strategy that most needs clarification. How can boundaries be iden-
tiffed empirically, without reference to distributions of organizational
characteristics?

of participants and publics than do previous approaches. The set of cat-

the location of social boundaries. The fact that virtually all historians
and observers of the American labor movement distinguish craft and
industrial unionism tells us something useful--that these are different
organizational forms. We suggest using such information, though it should
be recognized that conventional distinctions may persist for some time
after a boundary has ceased to exist.

DISCUSSION

       We have suggested that issues of classifying organizational popu-
lations be reformulated in dynamic terms by focusing on the processes
that create and destroy boundaries b e t w e e n populations. Such a shift in
focus has numerous operational implications, as w e have noted above.
Indeed, one possible advantage of this m o r e complex approach to clas-
sification problems is that it may turn what have b e e n mainly defini-
tional issues into researchable questions. Research that explores seg-
regating and blending processes in the organizational world in an effort
to identify populations with unit character will at the same time address
fundamental substantive questions. These questions, regarding the ef-
fects of technical change, social movements, and institutional processes,
lie at the core of attempts to understand the relation of organizational
processes to large-scale, long-term change.
       Explicit consideration of the dynamics of boundaries would also
help clarify the relation b e t w e e n institutional processes and selection
processes. Meyer and Scott ( 1 9 8 3 ) argue that there are two organiza-
tional w o r l d s - - o n e competitive, in which selection processes operate,
and one institutional, in which selection is replaced with processes of
symbolic isomorphism with institutional rules. The arguments in pre-
vious sections suggest a more complex relation between the two worlds.
Selection processes and institutional processes occur simultaneously and
can reinforce each other. Selection processes that result in sharp bound-

ization of organizational forms shapes selection processes, as we have
noted above. But institutionalization of a form or of an "organizational

at the boundary, greatly lessening tim life chances of non-institutional
forms attempting to enter the sector.
      The relationship between these two processes needs m u c h m o r e
attention. The thrust of the approach sketched here is to cast the issues
in dynamic terms. Institutional arguments often suggest that an insti-
tutionalized world will never change; institutional arguments lack mech-
anisms of change other than response to purely exogeneous forces. Un-
derstanding the opposing processes that sustain and erode the boundaries
around organizational populations is a first step in developing links be-
tween institutionalization and selection of organizational populations.
